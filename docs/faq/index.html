Working with me

What is a canon lawyer?

A canon lawyer is trained in the law of the Catholic Church — the Code of Canon Law and related norms — usually holding a Licentiate in Canon Law (JCL) from a pontifical faculty. Canon lawyers serve as judges, advocates, defenders of the bond and advisors within the Church’s legal system. A canon lawyer is not the same as a civil lawyer and does not advise on the law of the state.

Do I really need an advocate?

For a marriage nullity case, not always — many tribunals help parties directly. An advocate is most valuable when a case is complex, when you are unsure which grounds apply, or when you want someone to review the evidence and argue on your behalf. In penal cases and recourse against decrees, where rights and deadlines are strict, an advocate is strongly advisable; in a judicial penal trial it is required.

Do you work with clients outside Toronto?

Yes. Most of my work is done by phone, video and email, and I act for clients across Canada and the United States. To act as advocate before a particular tribunal, I need that tribunal’s approval, which I seek at the start of a case.

Marriage nullity

Is an annulment a “Catholic divorce”?

No. Divorce ends a marriage that exists. A declaration of nullity is a finding that, from the start, a valid marriage never came into being because something essential was missing. The tribunal examines the circumstances at the time of the wedding.

Does a declaration of nullity make my children illegitimate?

No. Children born of a marriage entered in good faith are legitimate, and remain so after a declaration of nullity (c. 1137). A declaration also has no effect on custody, support or other civil arrangements.

How long does a case take?

The law asks that a case be decided in first instance within about a year (c. 1453), and the briefer and documentary processes can be much faster. Actual timelines depend on the tribunal, the ground, and how quickly witnesses respond.

My former spouse filed a case. Do I have to participate?

No, but you have the right to — to give your account, name witnesses, review the evidence and appeal. Whether you support or oppose the petition, participating helps the tribunal reach the truth. I can help you decide how to respond.

Do I need a civil divorce first?

Generally, yes. Tribunals only accept a case once the marriage has irretrievably broken down (c. 1675), and in practice they usually ask for proof of civil divorce.

Penal cases and recourse

I’ve been told a preliminary investigation has begun. What should I do?

Get advice before you make any statement. You are presumed innocent (c. 1321 §1) and you are not required to incriminate yourself. If there is any possibility of civil or criminal proceedings, speak to a civil lawyer too.

My parish is being merged or closed. Can anything be done?

Possibly. Such decrees must follow specific procedures, and parishioners may be able to seek their revocation and bring hierarchical recourse to the Holy See. The first deadline is usually only ten useful days from notification of the decree, so contact me right away.

What are “useful days”?

Canon law counts many deadlines in useful days: days on which you knew about the decision and were able to act (c. 201 §2). They are not simply business days, and missing them usually means losing the right to challenge the decision.
