The Unprofitable Servant

A layman’s musing on the talents, for the thirty-third Sunday of the year, which this year is also the World Day of the Poor. Food for thought, not teaching.

Richard Verver · November 2026

Download PDF 8 pages

Who calls him hard

The Gospel this Sunday is the talents, and the Church has given the same Sunday to the poor: it is the tenth World Day of the Poor. I do not think the two landed together by accident. The Church is a mother, and she has a mother’s way of setting two things side by side on the table and saying nothing. I think we are being asked to read the parable from the back of the house, from the kitchen and the cot under the stairs, where the servants sleep.

Most of us learned it from the front. A rich man goes into a far country and leaves his goods with three servants, to every man according to his several ability. Two of them trade, and double what they were given. The third digs a hole. The lord comes home, praises the two, and throws the third out. We have heard it so often that the money has gone out of it. Our very word talent, for a knack at the piano or a head for figures, comes from this story. The lesson we draw is that God gives us gifts and wants them used. That lesson is true, and I mean to keep it. But a talent was silver, a man’s load of it, and I want to put the silver back.

A month ago, borrowing Chesterton’s voice for another reflection on this site, I called the third servant orthodox about the wrong God. I am less sure of that now. I have been looking at him since last night, and he will not stay where I put him.

Look at who calls the lord hard. Not the evangelist. The servant does, and he does it standing up, with the earth still on the silver: Lord, I knew thee that thou art an hard man, reaping where thou hast not sown, and gathering where thou hast not strawed. And the lord does not deny it. Thou knewest that I reap where I sowed not. The commentators say he is only taking the servant at his word, judging him out of his own mouth, as Luke has it. Perhaps. But reaping where you did not sow is not a figure of speech. It is rent. Other men plough the field and bend over it in the heat, and at harvest the owner’s wagon comes. The people who pick our lettuce and our grapes have never needed that verse explained to them.

What doubling cost

I wanted to know how a man in that world doubled a sum like this, and the answer is not comfortable. A talent was a weight before it was a sum: sixty pounds of silver and more, some six thousand days of a labourer’s wages. A working man could give twenty years for one. Josephus says Herod Antipas drew two hundred talents a year from all of Galilee and Perea. Five talents is a fortune, and the men handling it are the stewards of a great house.

There was no stock market to grow it in. There was lending, at a legal twelve in the hundred a year in the Roman world, and far more in the provinces. Cicero caught Brutus’s agents squeezing forty-eight in the hundred out of a town in Cyprus, and one of them had shut the town council in its own chamber, with cavalry at the door, until five of the councillors starved. There was lending to smallholders against their land, and taking the land when the harvest failed. There was buying the right to collect the taxes, and keeping whatever could be wrung out above the bid. There was trade by sea, which paid well because ships sank.

Nearly all of it came out of someone else’s body. Scripture remembers the sound of it. We have mortgaged our lands, vineyards, and houses, that we might buy corn, because of the dearth, the people cry to Nehemiah, and then the worst of it: we bring into bondage our sons and our daughters. That is what doubled silver is made of. Peasants took it for granted that the good things of the world were fixed in amount, so that when one man’s share doubled, another’s had shrunk. They were not far wrong. When the revolt broke out in the year 66, the rebels in Jerusalem burned the public archives, to destroy the moneylenders’ bonds. It tells you what the war was about, for a good many of them.

Our Bibles do not soften this. Thou oughtest therefore to have put my money to the exchangers, and then at my coming I should have received mine own with usury. Usury is the lord’s own word. And the man who wrote this Gospel down, as the Church has always said, once sat at the receipt of custom. Matthew had been a tax collector. He had held the ledger and seen the faces across the table. It is his Gospel that teaches us to pray, forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors. He knew exactly what reaping where you did not sow looks like, because he had done it, and had got up from the table and left it there. He is the last evangelist I would expect to use the phrase without meaning it.

The only clean way out

Then I asked what the third servant could have done instead, as a man bound by the Law of Moses. I am a canon lawyer, and it is the kind of question I cannot leave alone.

He could not lend the money at interest. Thou shalt not lend upon usury to thy brother. The rabbis later counted everyone who touches such a loan as a transgressor: the lender, the borrower, the guarantor, the witnesses, and the scribe who writes the bond. Carrying the money to the exchangers would have made him one of them.

He could not give it to the poor. This is the hard one, because it is what we would all have liked him to do. But it was not his to give. The Law speaks of the keeper who has put his hand unto his neighbour’s goods, and alms made of another man’s money are not alms. The rabbis would not let a man wave a stolen palm branch at the feast, and they cited Isaiah: I hate robbery for burnt offering. God does not want to be worshipped with what was taken. The Church would later teach that in urgent need the goods of the earth are common, and that bread taken for a starving man is not theft. But that is our law, not his. The rabbis held that even a man who saves his own life with another’s goods must pay for them. He was bound by the Law he had, and he kept it.

So he buried it. Think of the work. A talent is no coin to slip under a stone. He went out, I suppose after dark, with a spade and a load of silver on his back, and dug deep enough to keep it from the plough, and filled the hole in, and went home with earth under his nails. That was the recognised way to keep money safe; the Talmud says money is guarded only in the earth, and a keeper who buried a deposit was not liable for its loss. He found the one door the Law left open, and he went through it. Then, when the day came, he dug the silver up again, gave it back whole, and told his master to his face what kind of man he was. That is a lawyer’s obedience. It is also a protest, made with the back and the hands. And I do not think the ending surprised him. I knew thee, he says. A man who keeps the Law of Moses in such a house knows what the reckoning will be. He was afraid, he tells us, and he kept it anyway.

He reminds me of Susanna, cornered by the elders in the garden: I am straitened on every side: for if I do this thing, it is death unto me: and if I do it not, I cannot escape your hands. She chose to fall into their hands rather than sin. Our Lord liked this sort of exactness. Turn the other cheek, give the cloak as well, go the second mile: as Walter Wink reads them, each keeps the rule to the letter and leaves the shame with the man enforcing it.

Dorothy Day did it in 1960. The City of New York took the Catholic Worker’s house at 223 Chrystie Street for a subway, paid $68,700 for it, and then, a year and a half on, sent $3,579.39 in interest on the award. That had been a house of hospitality, with a line of hungry men at the door. She knew to the bowl what $3,579.39 would buy. It was not usury in the old sense: the city owed it for paying late, and no law, the Church’s or the city’s, asked her to refuse it. She sent it back anyway. Her letter, printed in the paper as “This Money Is Not Ours”, said plainly, “We do not believe in the profit system,” and rested the work of the house on Matthew 25. Readers wrote in to criticise her, and she answered them in the paper. When the city said it would spend the money on buying more property, she asked that it go to the city’s own radio station, WNYC, instead. Lo, there thou hast that is thine.

Hid in the earth

Matthew has a word for what the third servant did, and it is not a bad word. I was afraid, and went and hid thy talent in the earth. In Matthew, hiding a thing in the ground is what the kingdom itself does. The kingdom of heaven is like unto treasure hid in a field; the which when a man hath found, he hideth. It is like leaven which a woman took, and hid in three measures of meal. The same Greek word for hiding runs through all three.

There is a joke in it, too. The servant says his lord reaps where he did not sow. Yet the servant is the only man in the story who puts anything into the ground.

I write this in the middle of November. In this part of the world the fields look dead, and the winter wheat is already under them. Nobody who farms thinks a buried thing is a wasted thing.

Only Matthew reads the sign of Jonas as a burial: so shall the Son of man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth. And the parable opens with a lord who delivered unto them his goods, while the next chapter opens with the Son of man is betrayed to be crucified. Our Bibles use two English words there. Matthew uses one Greek verb: handed over. Two chapters on, a rich man takes the body, wraps it in clean linen, and lays it in his own new tomb, which he had hewn out in the rock. The treasure of heaven is hid in the earth, and a great stone is rolled to the door.

Someone will say that a buried seed bears fruit, and this talent came back exactly as it went in. I think that is the point. Aristotle called interest the most unnatural kind of gain, because the Greek word for it also means offspring: money breeding money. The schoolmen took this up. Money does not breed. Bury money and you dig up money. Only living things rise multiplied. The servant told the truth about what money is, and he told it with a spade.

The seed in this story is not the coin. It is the man. He is the one who goes into the dark. And here St John gives the word the whole parable has been waiting for: The hour is come, that the Son of man should be glorified. Verily, verily, I say unto you, Except a corn of wheat fall into the ground and die, it abideth alone: but if it die, it bringeth forth much fruit. Glorified. In St John the glory and the burial are one hour. The traders doubled their money. In the parable of the sower, the good ground gives some an hundredfold, some sixtyfold, some thirtyfold. The earth is a better banker than the exchangers.

St Paul says that we carry this treasure in earthen vessels. We are the earth the treasure is hid in. God hid his own treasure in flesh, first in a girl of Nazareth, whose flesh he made his own, and then in us, by grace, which is another way of being hid. He does not seem to think it wasted in either. For ye are dead, St Paul says again, and your life is hid with Christ in God. It is the same word. What is hid is not lost. It is waiting for its hour.

Two masters

Matthew has already told us there are two lords to choose between. No man can serve two masters: for either he will hate the one, and love the other; or else he will hold to the one, and despise the other. Ye cannot serve God and mammon. The word for serve there is a slave’s word. Read it again beside today’s Gospel. A man who holds to the one will despise the other. The third servant despised his lord to his face. Our Lord had said he would.

And this chapter sets two masters side by side, each with his own reckoning. The lord of the talents counts silver, and rewards the servants who doubled it. The King on the throne, in the next scene, counts cups of water and visits to the sick, and rewards people who did not know they were serving him at all.

Our Bibles leave room for this. In the Authorized Version the words the kingdom of heaven is, at the head of the parable, are printed in italics: the translators supplied them. The Greek says only “For as a man,” and never finishes the comparison. The translators had good reason, since the chapter began with the kingdom. Still, Matthew did not write that the kingdom is like this man. The Fathers read the lord as Christ, and I do not set them aside. In the letter of the story his house is mammon’s; in its figure the Lord comes to judge. Matthew can hold both.

If the lord is mammon, the first two servants are his priests, and the counting table is his altar. Well done, he says, and enter thou into the joy of thy lord, which is the language of worship. But look at what the joy turns out to be. Thou hast been faithful over a few things, I will make thee ruler over many things. More silver to mind. Anyone who has worked for mammon knows that promotion. They have their reward, as our Lord says of the hypocrites who sound a trumpet before their alms. Mammon pays at once, and in his own coin.

This does not cancel the old lesson; it sharpens it. After the unjust steward our Lord says that the children of this world are in their generation wiser than the children of light. If mammon’s priests will lose sleep to double his money for him, what will we do for the true Lord, who asks for a cup of cold water?

Not every lord in the parables is mammon. The bridegroom of last Sunday’s Gospel is Jesus, and there is nothing more to say about that.

The darkness outside

And cast ye the unprofitable servant into outer darkness: there shall be weeping and gnashing of teeth. The Greek says, more simply, the darkness outside: outside the lit house, at night, where the lamps of the feast do not reach. For a household slave in that world, being put out meant falling among the landless, the day labourers and the beggars. Luke’s unjust steward names the fear exactly: I cannot dig; to beg I am ashamed. Our servant has already shown that he can dig.

I do not have to imagine the darkness outside. It is November in my city, and there are people in it tonight, in doorways and in tents in the ravines. The weeping is real. And anyone who has stood an hour in a November wind knows what the teeth do.

There is an older way out of a house by night. The LORD thy God brought thee forth out of Egypt by night. Read so, the servant leaves mammon’s house no longer his slave, and poor, as Israel was poor in the wilderness, living on manna, with a pillar of fire for a lamp. The Law even says how a master must let a slave go: thou shalt not let him go away empty, but furnish him liberally out of thy flock, and out of thy floor, and out of thy winepress. This lord takes back the one talent and hands it to the man who has ten. The servant kept the Law to the end, and his lord’s last act breaks it.

Now listen to the names the lord calls this man. Wicked: blessed are ye when men shall say all manner of evil against you falsely. Luke’s version is closer still: blessed when they cast out your name as evil. Unprofitable: that is the name our Lord tells his own disciples to give themselves. We are unprofitable servants: we have done that which was our duty to do. Cast: the tenants of the vineyard take the son, and cast him out of the vineyard, and slew him. Every word thrown at him is a word the Gospel has already made holy.

The lord’s closing proverb reads differently from the back of the house, too. For unto every one that hath shall be given, and he shall have abundance: but from him that hath not shall be taken away even that which he hath. Sociologists call this the Matthew effect. It is not a law of grace. It is how money works.

Then read straight on, as Matthew wrote it, without stopping where the lectionary stops. The servant is put outside into the dark. The very next words are these: When the Son of man shall come in his glory, and all the holy angels with him, then shall he sit upon the throne of his glory. Darkness, and then glory, one verse apart. And when the glory opens its mouth, it has a body, and the body is in want: I was an hungred, and ye gave me meat: I was thirsty, and ye gave me drink: I was a stranger, and ye took me in: Naked, and ye clothed me. He names himself among them. Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me.

Then: after two days is the feast of the passover, and the Son of man is betrayed to be crucified. He dies outside the city, stripped, and from the sixth hour there was darkness over all the land. The Epistle to the Hebrews draws the moral: Jesus also, that he might sanctify the people with his own blood, suffered without the gate. Let us go forth therefore unto him without the camp, bearing his reproach.

I know the objections. Matthew uses weeping and gnashing of teeth six times, always of judgment, and once, a chapter earlier, of a servant who beats his fellow servants. In the Psalms it is the wicked who gnash their teeth. Luke’s version has no darkness at all. I do not say Matthew did not mean judgment. I say a tax collector can mean two things at once.

Someone will also say the servant gave nothing to the poor; he only refused, and handed the money back. But the refusal was his act, and he chose it with his eyes open, knowing the lord and knowing the price. Our Lord says of his own life, No man taketh it from me, but I lay it down of myself. The Passion is undergone and chosen at once. What I took last month for a man standing still beside a hole was obedience, and it has the shape of the cross.

The holy usury

None of this overturns the old reading. It tells us what the gift is for. The tradition always knew of one lawful usury: He that hath pity upon the poor lendeth unto the LORD; and that which he hath given will he pay him again. St Basil, preaching against the moneylenders, told them that if they must have interest, the Lord would pay it through the poor. St Augustine put it more sharply: “The self-same Lord, who bade thee not lend on usury, comes forward as the Person to whom thou shouldest lend on usury!”

Today’s first reading is the lord of the parable turned inside out. The valiant woman’s husband trusts her, so that he shall have no need of spoil. She does not reap where she has not sown: with the fruit of her hands she planteth a vineyard. She is up before anyone, while it is yet night, and not to count. She giveth meat to her household, and a portion to her maidens. In the middle of all her trading, She stretcheth out her hand to the poor; yea, she reacheth forth her hands to the needy. There is no darkness outside her house, for her candle goeth not out by night. She is not afraid of the snow, the chapter says; she is a woman that feareth the LORD, and that fear has cast out the other. And she is paid in her own coin: Give her of the fruit of her hands. Dorothy Day was such a woman, and every soup kitchen I have heard of is kept by another.

The psalm is the third servant’s blessing. For thou shalt eat the labour of thine hands: happy shalt thou be, and it shall be well with thee. A slave had no house and no table. The psalm gives a free man both: a wife as a fruitful vine by the sides of thine house, children like olive plants round about thy table. It is a small picture: bread a man earned himself, and faces round it. It is a song for pilgrims going up to Jerusalem, and it sounds like Micah: they shall sit every man under his vine and under his fig tree; and none shall make them afraid. Peter Maurin asked for little more than this. He wanted a world where it was easier to be good, and the Catholic Worker kept a farm in his name.

The sheep in the next scene did their trading in bread and water and visits to the prison. It is not easy trading. Dostoevsky’s elder says that “love in action is a harsh and dreadful thing compared with love in dreams,” and those who keep the soup lines say he was right. But that is the doubling the King counts. So the old moral stands: do not bury the gift. But the gift is mercy, and its profit is laid up with the least. The third servant would not lend at usury, and he was put out among the very people the holy usury is lent to.

Pope Leo’s message for today says the poor of our time are “robbed not only of bread, but also of a voice and a face.” The third servant had a voice. He used it once, to his master’s face, and was put outside. The Pope took his theme from the Psalms, the LORD is his refuge, the same psalm that speaks of the workers of iniquity who eat up my people as they eat bread.

It came to me this morning that many of the parables of the kingdom are the beatitudes in their shadow form. The beatitudes say whose the kingdom is: theirs is the kingdom of heaven, at the beginning and again at the end. The parables say what it is like, and often they tell it from outside, where the blessed look lost. Matthew sets a beatitude among his parables to show how to read them: But blessed are your eyes, for they see. The man thrown out today is the man of the beatitudes, seen from mammon’s side of the door. Next Sunday, the feast of Christ the King, the same chapter steps into the light: Come, ye blessed of my Father, inherit the kingdom.

I am a layman, and this is a musing, not doctrine. I do not say the outer darkness is not real, or that a man may bury what God has given him. I only notice that the servant the world calls unprofitable bears the name our Lord gave his own. And I notice where the darkness outside leads, if you keep reading. It leads out of the city, to a hill under a darkened sky, and then to a new tomb, where the King of the next scene is hid in the earth. If that is where they threw the servant, they threw him towards his Lord.

And we know what the earth did with what was hid in it that time. It is sown in dishonour; it is raised in glory.

To watch Billy Wilder’s The Apartment (1960) is the third servant at a desk. A clerk rises in a great insurance house by serving his masters’ pleasures, and is paid in mammon’s coin: a promotion, an office, a key to the executive washroom. On New Year’s Eve he hands the key back, lo, there thou hast that is thine, and walks out with nothing. Frank Capra’s It’s a Wonderful Life (1946) has the two lords in one small town: Mr Potter, who reaps where he did not sow, and a building and loan that lends to the poor and never grows rich. On television, Severance (2022–) is mammon’s house with its liturgy intact: servants minding numbers they do not understand, and a joy of their lord that turns out to be a waffle party.
