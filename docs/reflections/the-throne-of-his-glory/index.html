Reflection · a layman’s reading

The Throne of His Glory

The sheep and the goats, for the feast of Christ the King, the last Sunday of the year. Who is speaking, where the throne stands, what glory weighs, a King who reigns in death, and the respectable people on his left. Food for thought, not teaching; the liberties are marked where they are taken.

Richard Verver · November 2026

Download PDF 10 pages

Who is speaking

Abraham Joshua Heschel said that the prophets do not speak the way the rest of us do. What is slight to us, a man cheated, a poor family put out of doors, is to them a disaster. The prophet is not a man with a better argument. He is a man who cannot sleep. I have no such gift. But I want to read today’s Gospel as if I had been kept awake by it, because the man who spoke it was.

Three Sundays running, the Church has read us the twenty-fifth chapter of Matthew: the ten virgins, the talents, and now the sheep and the goats. It is the end of the last long teaching our Lord gives before he dies. He gives it sitting on the Mount of Olives, looking across the valley at the Temple, and the next words out of his mouth are these: Ye know that after two days is the feast of the passover, and the Son of man is betrayed to be crucified. Whatever this parable is, it is not a lecture on the end of the world. It was told by a man who had two days.

Listen to who is speaking, because it is stranger than it sounds. Matthew is telling us what Jesus said. Jesus is telling his friends about someone he calls the Son of man, as if of another: When the Son of man shall come in his glory. Three verses later, without warning, the Son of man has become the King: Then shall the King say unto them on his right hand. The King speaks of someone else again: Come, ye blessed of my Father. And then the King says me. He says it over and over. And me turns out to be the hungry, the thirsty, the stranger, the naked, the sick and the prisoner: Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me.

Five names in fifteen verses: Jesus, the Son of man, the King, the Father, and the least. The one telling the story is in it, on the throne. He is also, by his own word, in the crowd before the throne, and in the worst-dressed part of it. Daniel had seen one like the Son of man come with the clouds and be given dominion, and glory, and a kingdom. In Daniel the kingdom is given him by the Ancient of days. In the parable he calls the giver my Father.

I do not want to tidy this up. It is the life of the Trinity told as a story: the Son speaking of the Father, and naming himself in his brethren. But notice what it does to the trial. Every court I have sat in keeps the judge and the injured party apart. This court does not. The judge says me. Heschel spent his life saying that the God of the prophets is not indifferent: what is done to the poor is not nothing to him. The Church says that God does not change, and that can sound like the opposite. It is not. It means there was never a time when he did not care; the cross did not make him merciful. St John calls our Lord the Lamb slain from the foundation of the world. Nothing in God was altered on Calvary. The love that was in him before there was a world was written, on a Friday, under Pontius Pilate, into flesh; and flesh can be wounded. So here the Son of man says it in the first person. The King on the throne is the plaintiff, and the wound is his: in his own flesh on that Friday, and, by his own word, in the least.

The throne is a cross

Then shall he sit upon the throne of his glory. Where is that throne? Matthew has used the phrase once before, when our Lord promised the twelve that they would sit on twelve thrones when the Son of man shall sit in the throne of his glory. One chapter later a mother comes to him with her two sons and asks for the seats beside it: Grant that these my two sons may sit, the one on thy right hand, and the other on the left, in thy kingdom.

His answer is a riddle. Ye know not what ye ask. Are ye able to drink of the cup that I shall drink of? And then: to sit on my right hand, and on my left, is not mine to give, but it shall be given to them for whom it is prepared of my Father. Prepared of my Father: the very words the King will use of the kingdom.

Seven chapters on, we find out for whom the seats were prepared. Then were there two thieves crucified with him, one on the right hand, and another on the left. Over his head they nail his title: THIS IS JESUS THE KING OF THE JEWS. The soldiers have already given him his regalia: a scarlet robe, a crown of thorns, a reed for a sceptre in his right hand, and a court kneeling in mockery, Hail, King of the Jews! The passers-by make the coronation speech: If he be the King of Israel, let him now come down from the cross, and we will believe him. He does not come down. That is the King’s answer. A king who came down would be the kind of king they wanted.

Now look at him there, and count. He is thirsty: I thirst, he says, and they give him vinegar. He is naked: they have parted his garments, casting lots. He is a prisoner, bound in the garden and never loosed. He is sick, if a man scourged and nailed is sick; Matthew has already said of him, Himself took our infirmities, and bare our sicknesses. He is a stranger, put to death outside the gate of his own city; He came unto his own, and his own received him not. He has eaten nothing since the supper. Every word the King will say from the throne, I was an hungred … I was thirsty … I was a stranger … naked … sick … in prison, is true of this man on this afternoon. The parable is not a riddle about where Christ might be hiding. It is a description of Friday, given two days early.

And this, St John says, is the glory. The hour is come, that the Son of man should be glorified. And I, if I be lifted up from the earth, will draw all men unto me. This he said, signifying what death he should die. We have made glory mean brightness: gold, trumpets, a crowd on its feet. The Hebrew word is heavier. Kavod means weight. Glory is what a thing weighs when it is put in the balance. When Moses begged, I beseech thee, shew me thy glory, the answer was not a blaze. It was: I will make all my goodness pass before thee. God’s glory is his goodness, seen. It was never seen so plainly as on the day it had a body, and the body was stripped.

So when the Son of man comes in his glory, I do not think he comes in some other glory than this one. Earlier in this same speech he says that then shall appear the sign of the Son of man in heaven, and the Fathers took the sign to be the cross. St John saw the throne of heaven, and in the midst of it a Lamb as it had been slain. The risen Lord kept his wounds, and showed them. He will not have laid them down to judge us. The throne of his glory is a cross, and the first two to sit beside it in his kingdom were thieves.

What does a king see from such a throne? Not what kings see. A king on a dais looks out over heads to the horizon. A man on a cross looks down. He sees his mother. He sees the soldiers dicing for his coat. He sees the tops of the heads of the people who have come to watch. From that throne the least are not far off, at the back of the hall. They are the nearest thing there is. That is the glory as it is seen from the cross: the whole world, from above, at the height of a man’s nailed feet.

Regnavit a ligno

Christus vincit, Christus regnat, Christus imperat. Christ conquers, Christ reigns, Christ commands. It is the opening of the Laudes Regiae, the royal acclamations, old enough that some form of them may have been sung when Charlemagne was crowned on Christmas Day in the year 800, and the Church still sings them when a pope begins his ministry. It is a glorious chant, and it is the Church’s.

Lately it has been carried on banners, chanted at rallies and typed into the internet, in Latin and in English, as Christ is King. Many who say it mean what every Christian means. Some mean a nation: that this country, its borders and its power, is where Christ’s reign is to be seen. I will not read anyone’s heart. I only want to ask the parable where the King sits, and then ask whether the banner is being carried toward that place or away from it.

The feast has a history that helps. Pius XI instituted it in 1925, in Quas primas, and set it on the last Sunday of October. He did teach that Christ has authority over civil affairs too, and I will not pretend otherwise. But the same letter says plainly that this kingdom is spiritual and is concerned with spiritual things, and recalls that when the crowd would have acclaimed him King, he shrank from the honour. It even warns against greed dressed up as public spirit and patriotism. In 1969 Paul VI renamed the feast Our Lord Jesus Christ, King of the Universe, and moved it to the last Sunday of the year, to make its meaning about the end of all things clearer. The Church took the feast out of October and set it at the edge of time, where no flag has ever been planted.

The Gospels know two kinds of king, and they put them in one room. On the morning of the Passion, Herod Antipas had Jesus brought before him. I wrote about that man in another essay: a ruler of a quarter who once swore away the half of my kingdom at a dinner, having no kingdom, and paid the oath with a prophet’s head. That morning he dressed the Son of man in a gorgeous robe for a joke and sent him back. One king promises half of what he does not own, to be seen promising. The other owns everything, and we shall see what he does with it. One wants the noise that power makes. The other answered him nothing.

And the Church has her own answer to where he reigns. In Passiontide she sings Venantius Fortunatus’s hymn to the cross, Vexilla regis prodeunt: the banners of the King go forth. So there are banners. But listen to what is on them. One verse says that what David sang has been fulfilled, dicendo nationibus: regnavit a ligno Deus. He said to the nations: God has reigned from the tree. The old Latin psalter read from the tree in the psalm, The Lord hath reigned, and St Justin accused the rabbis of cutting the words out. Whether or not the words were ever there, the Church kept them. Christus regnat, yes. A ligno. From the wood. The King’s banner is the thing he was nailed to.

Notice who stands before the throne in the parable. Before him shall be gathered all nations. Not one nation, and not the nations ranked. All of them, as nations, in the dock. And the test he sets them includes this: I was a stranger, and ye took me in. The Law of Moses had said it first, and grounded it in memory: for ye know the heart of a stranger, seeing ye were strangers in the land of Egypt. I have written elsewhere about a grandmother of seventy-three who went to a federal building to sign her name, as she had every six months for thirteen years, and did not come home; in the cell where they held her she asked for water to take her medicine, and a guard gave her a plate of ice. I was thirsty. I do not say who that guard is before God. Heschel said that in a free society few are guilty, but all are responsible, and that is closer to what the parable says: it is the nations that are gathered. But I do say that a Christus regnat shouted over a plate of ice has not yet found the throne it is shouting about. The King it names has told us on which side of the bars to look for him.

King in death

Here is the thought that set this reflection going. If the throne is a cross, then the Son of man begins to reign by dying. And a King who reigns by dying has gone where every one of his subjects must go. No other king has. Kings send men to die. The Epistle to the Hebrews says that this one came that he by the grace of God should taste death for every man, and that through death he might destroy him that had the power of death, and deliver those who through fear of death were all their lifetime subject to bondage. He did not watch death from a throne. Death was the throne. He tasted it, and Hebrews does not say for mankind. It says for every man: for each one.

The creed says he descended into hell, and St Peter says he went and preached unto the spirits in prison. Set that beside the parable. I was in prison, and ye came unto me. He did for the dead what the sheep did for him: he visited the prisoners. And St John gives the reason he is judge at all. The Father hath given him authority to execute judgment also, because he is the Son of man. Not because he is strong. Because he is one of us, and has been where we go. The judge is the one who died. The hour is coming, and now is, when the dead shall hear the voice of the Son of God: and they that hear shall live.

Now the liberty, marked as mine. I think that when each of us dies, we find that he has been there before us. Today’s psalm says it of every valley: Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil: for thou art with me. And I think that is where the judgment happens. The Church teaches as much in her own way: each soul is judged at once, at death, and goes to its reward or its purification or its loss without waiting for the end of the world. The last day, when the nations are gathered and the dead rise with their bodies, does not give a second verdict. It shows in the light, before everyone, what each death already was.

We picture the two judgments as stations on a railway line: my death at one station, then years of track, then the last day far down the line. That picture is a limit of our imagining, not of the thing. On the King’s side there is one judging, and it is not on the tracks at all; God does not do things at a date. On ours it touches us twice, in our dying and in our rising, and those two keep their order: the soul first, and the body after, waiting. That is why the Church guards the Assumption of Our Lady as a privilege that anticipates ours, not the common rule. What falls away is the clock between them, not the order. Anyone who uses git knows the shape. A history is a web of commits, and git log has to print it as a list, one line under another, because a screen scrolls in one direction. The list is the log’s limitation, not the history’s. In a branch that is merged, the judgment at death and the rising at the end are one merge, printed on two lines.

And what will it be like? If I read the parable right, it will be a recognition. Both groups say the same thing: Lord, when saw we thee? Neither knew whom they were feeding or failing to feed. In death we see the face.

And in death we are, every one of us, the least. Naked came I out of my mother’s womb, and naked shall I return thither. Go into any room where someone is dying and count again. They cannot eat. Their lips are wet with a sponge, as his were. They are strangers already, half gone into a country none of us has seen. They are stripped of everything that was theirs: their work, their strength, their name on a door. They are sick, and they are shut in. The six works are done at every deathbed in the world, by daughters and nurses and nuns, or they are not done. And the King who said I was all six of those things tasted that death before any of us did. The face we meet there is the face of the one we fed. Or the face of the one we did not.

The parents’ school

There is a class of people who know the King’s six works by heart, because they do them before breakfast: parents. Feed the hungry, at three in the morning, again. Give drink to the thirsty, who has thrown the cup on the floor. Clothe the naked: every child arrives naked, and has to be clothed, and then clothed again when the clothes are outgrown in a month. Take in the stranger: a newborn is a stranger, a person you have never met, who moves into your house and is given the best room in it. Visit the sick: the long nights of fevers, a hand on a hot forehead, the floor beside the bed. Some parents learn the sixth work too, at a visiting window, and keep coming.

And almost none of them think of it as serving Christ. They think of it as Tuesday. That is exactly the sheep’s surprise: Lord, when saw we thee an hungred, and fed thee? They are not being modest. They truly do not remember anything worth a kingdom. The parable is full of people who did the works without knowing whom they did them for, and a house with small children in it is full of such people, too tired to notice that they are standing at the right hand.

Matthew has already told us who the least are likely to be. Whoso shall receive one such little child in my name receiveth me. He calls them the little ones, and says a cup of cold water given to one of them will not lose its reward, and says something stranger: in heaven their angels do always behold the face of my Father. The glory is turned toward the smallest person in the room.

Here is something about the King that parents can teach the rest of us. A child receives everything and gives back, for years, nothing you could put a price on. The world ranks the one who receives below the one who gives; it calls him a burden, a dependant, a cost. The King does not. He names himself in the one who receives. And that is no accident, because it is his own life: the Son receives everything he has from the Father, for as the Father hath life in himself; so hath he given to the Son to have life in himself. To receive is not the lower place. It is the Son’s place. Whoever despises the one who can only receive has despised the form of the Son.

I must not make this a ranking, as if parents were nearer the throne than anyone else. Every vocation has its least. The nurse on the night shift has them, and the priest at a bedside, and the teacher with the one child nobody else can manage, and the single woman who takes in her sister’s children, and the religious sister who has no children of her own and has fed more strangers than most of us ever will. Marriage and the begetting of children are one great school of the six works. They are not the only one, and they are not a higher one. Their dignity is that the King’s works are their daily bread, and that nobody applauds.

The right hand

The first sentence ever passed from that throne is in St Luke. One of the two thieves turns his head and says, Lord, remember me when thou comest into thy kingdom. He alone, that afternoon, asks the dying man for a kingdom. He has nothing left to give: no bread, no cup, no coat, and his hands are nailed. He gives the one work he can still reach. He speaks up for the prisoner beside him: this man hath done nothing amiss. And the King answers from the wood: To day shalt thou be with me in paradise. That is the right hand. It was a thief’s, and it took one sentence.

What happens to the sheep is the resurrection. Today’s second reading says it in order: But now is Christ risen from the dead, and become the firstfruits of them that slept. … For as in Adam all die, even so in Christ shall all be made alive. And then St Paul gives us his own Christus regnat: For he must reign, till he hath put all enemies under his feet. The last enemy that shall be destroyed is death. That is what his reign is for. It is not a reign against a people, or a party, or the stranger at the border. It is a reign against death, and death is the last enemy it puts under his feet.

And then the strangest thing of all. The King does not keep the kingdom. He hands it over, to God, even the Father, so that God may be all in all. It is the opposite of every throne I know of. Herod swore away half of nothing to be admired. This King, to whom everything has been given, gives all of it, to be seen by no one but his Father. Some in the fourth century read this verse too fast and said his reign would end, and the Creed answered them: of whose kingdom there shall be no end. He hands it over and does not lose it, the way one flame lights another and burns no less. That is what glory does. It gives itself away and is not made smaller.

The sheep are welcomed into a kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world. It was theirs before anything was. St John uses the same words of the Lamb. The kingdom and the cross were prepared together. And Isaiah had told them how the light would come. Is it not to deal thy bread to the hungry, and that thou bring the poor that are cast out to thy house? when thou seest the naked, that thou cover him; and that thou hide not thyself from thine own flesh? Then shall thy light break forth as the morning. The prophet promises glory to the works of mercy in so many words: the glory of the LORD shall be thy rereward. It will come up behind you. You will not see it, any more than the sheep did.

The first reading tells us what kind of shepherd sets them there. I will seek that which was lost, and bring again that which was driven away, and will bind up that which was broken, and will strengthen that which was sick. That is the six works again, done by God himself. The King asks of his sheep nothing that the Shepherd has not first done for them.

The left hand

And the goats. I want to be careful here, because the parable is.

Then shall he say also unto them on the left hand, Depart from me, ye cursed, into everlasting fire, prepared for the devil and his angels. Read it beside the welcome. The kingdom was prepared for you from the foundation of the world. The fire was not prepared for them at all. It was prepared for the devil and his angels. No human being was ever made for it. And yet the last verse says, these shall go away into everlasting punishment: but the righteous into life eternal, and I will not unsay what the text says.

I am a man who has spent too long around software, and I find myself thinking of it as a branch in git. The sheep are merged into the main line: their history becomes part of the King’s. The goats’ branch is not merged. But it is not deleted either. Nothing they were is erased; every commit stands. And both branches live in one repository. Remember St John’s name for him: the Lamb slain from the foundation of the world. The passion has a date, like any commit. But it is also, so to speak, the git init: the act the whole history was begun in. No branch, merged or unmerged, is outside it. Thou lovest all the things that are, and abhorrest nothing which thou hast made. St Thomas thought that even there mercy is at work, since God punishes less than is deserved. What else the Lord of that branch does with it, the parable does not say, and I stop where it stops.

But I cannot stop before I have looked at who they are, because this is the part that keeps me awake. Look at what the goats are condemned for. Not one thing they did. Only what they did not do. For I was an hungred, and ye gave me no meat: I was thirsty, and ye gave me no drink: I was a stranger, and ye took me not in: naked, and ye clothed me not: sick, and in prison, and ye visited me not. There is no murder in the list. No theft, no adultery, no cruelty. Not one of the things we mean when we say sinner.

The goats are not the villains of a melodrama. I think they are respectable people: just, as the world counts justice; prudent; careful with money; and quite sure that the poor should work harder. They paid what they owed. They broke no law. They had reasons, and the reasons were good ones: the man would only drink it; the woman should not have come here; there are agencies for that. That frightens me far more than wickedness does. Evil is easy to see, and easy to refuse. And nobody sets out to be a goat. St Thomas held that no one wills evil as evil; the will moves only toward some good, or what looks like one. The goats loved goods: order, desert, prudence, a sound budget, a quiet street. Two Sundays ago it was the wise virgins clutching their flasks. Last Sunday, a lord counting his silver. Prudence keeps turning up in these parables on the wrong side of the door.

The prophets knew these people, and feared them more than they feared the wicked. Ezekiel says what the sin of Sodom was, and it is not what we think: pride, fulness of bread, and abundance of idleness was in her and in her daughters, neither did she strengthen the hand of the poor and needy. Amos does not cry woe on the violent. He cries it on the comfortable: Woe to them that are at ease in Zion, who are not grieved for the affliction of Joseph. Not grieved. That is the whole charge. And today’s first reading, if we read a few verses past where the lectionary stops, tells us what a goat is. Behold, I, even I, will judge between the fat cattle and between the lean cattle. Because ye have thrust with side and with shoulder, and pushed all the diseased with your horns. Seemeth it a small thing unto you to have eaten up the good pasture? The goats of the parable are Ezekiel’s fat sheep. They did nothing but eat well, and take up room.

Heschel said that indifference to evil is worse than evil itself, and that the opposite of good is not evil but indifference. The parable agrees with him to the letter. The left hand is not filled with those who hated the King. It is filled with those who did not notice him, and were not troubled that they did not.

Nor can I say the goats failed because they did not see, since the sheep did not see either. Both ask the same question: Lord, when saw we thee? Even at the cross it was so: Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do. Nobody sees. The difference is that the sheep, not seeing, loved. St Peter has the words for it: Whom having not seen, ye love. That is the blessing of the right hand. It is not sight, which none of them had. It is love that did not wait for it.

Come, ye blessed

For two Sundays the Church read us parables in shadow: a door shut on five women with no oil, a servant put out into the dark. I suggested last week that many of the parables of the kingdom are the beatitudes seen from outside, where the blessed look lost. Today the beatitude steps into the light and says its own name. Come, ye blessed of my Father, inherit the kingdom. Blessed, and the kingdom: the first words of the Sermon on the Mount, spoken at last from the throne. And the voice that says them is the voice that said them on the hillside. He has not changed his mind about the poor.

I am a layman, and this is a musing, not doctrine. I do not say that the last day is nothing but our deaths added up, or that the left hand is empty, or that anyone may sit loose to the works because the King is kind. I only notice where the throne is. It is a cross, with a thief on either side. I notice what glory weighs: a body, stripped, that would not come down. And I notice where the King says he is to be looked for: in the hungry, the stranger and the prisoner, and in the death that waits for each of us, which he tasted first. If that is his throne, his reign does not come by any nation’s power, and never did. It comes the way it came on Golgotha. Regnavit a ligno Deus.

So what is to be done? Not a programme. A cup of water. The parable is almost insulting in how little it asks. No one at the right hand ended hunger. They fed someone. They did not empty the prisons. They went. St Teresa of Calcutta used to say that she was not called to be successful, but faithful, and the six works are the size of a pair of hands on purpose. Somewhere within a mile of where I am writing there is a person who is thirsty tonight, or a stranger, or shut in. The King has told me, two days before his death, that he will take it personally.

Next Sunday the Church begins her year again, and the King of the Universe will be waited for as a child. He will arrive naked and hungry, and there will be no room for him: a stranger. The last word of the year and the first are the same word. I was a stranger, and ye took me in. The glory of the Lord is coming up behind whoever does.
