The Benedict Year

The Benedict Year

Montessori and the atrium

Montessori shapes the day and how mistakes are found; the Catechesis of the Good Shepherd shapes the year’s prayer. Neither keeps score.

Download PDF 4 pages

Maria Montessori shapes how the day runs and how mistakes are found. The Catechesis of the Good Shepherd shapes the year’s prayer and its God and Worship units. Both refuse prizes. Montessori called prizes and punishments “the bench of the soul” (The Montessori Method, 1912, chapter I), and the Catechesis asks the catechist to give up quizzes, tests and exams, because Christ is the only teacher. That is why they fit Mr. Benedict: none of them keeps score.

The work period

Each morning is one long work period, uninterrupted. The Association Montessori Internationale’s elementary standard is three hours, though its own answer is that there is no magic in the number three: the rule is not to break in on the child. The day’s Benedict presentation opens the morning. Then the child chooses from the shelf: the week’s mathematics, the unit’s materials, the essay, his own writing.

Control of error

Montessori built her materials so that the material itself “contains within itself the control of errors” (chapter XXII). The cylinder that will not fit its socket shows the mistake, and no adult has to. The book’s problems need the same thing, and the answer key, used by the child himself, is the simplest form of it. Mathematics runs in three steps: from the material, to the book’s problem, to the child checking his own answer.

The Great Stories

Montessori elementary classrooms open each year with five stories, told to spark research rather than to teach details. They fall on the map. The Story of the Universe (“God Who Has No Hands”, called the grand overture to cosmic education) opens the year on its first day, as Montessori classrooms do. The other four come with their units.

The atrium

The Catechesis’s Level III (ages 9 to 12) centres on the Plan of God. The atrium meets at least once a week for two hours or more, led by a parent formed in the Catechesis. Nothing in it is measured, and the Friday trial never enters it. Its presentations fall on the map too.

The Catechesis’s levels end at 12, so for a 12- or 13-year-old this is the atrium’s last year. It is not a published curriculum, and its finished materials are not sold: the presentations are given by someone formed in them (Level III formation is about 110 to 120 hours, after Levels I and II), and the materials are made by hand.

The adult

Montessori’s adult prepares the environment and observes. The catechist is “the useless servant” (Luke 17:10) and listens with the child. So Mr. Benedict’s tests change hands: the parent notes what the child’s reasons show, and nothing is marked.

Going out, and the land

In 10 The City and 11 Law the child plans a going-out the Montessori way: he finds what his work needs, arranges the visit himself, and reports back. A council meeting, the parish office, a courtroom. For 12 and up, Montessori’s Erdkinder (“earth children”) pictured adolescents farming, keeping a shop and a guest house, and taking machines apart in a “museum of machinery”. A small stall with one posted price is Unit 9’s essay put to work: a market you could send a child to.
