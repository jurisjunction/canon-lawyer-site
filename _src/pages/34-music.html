A New Song

Some of the essays have a track you can play, and each track quotes the tracks it comes from. Here they sing together. There is a motet, where the chant they all share holds the bottom and the younger tunes sing above it, and a household, one file that keeps every track, to play one or stack them.

O sing unto the LORD a new song; for he hath done marvellous things.
— Psalm 98:1

Homo novus novit canticum novum.
A new man knows a new song.
— St Augustine, Sermon 34

Since Whether the Cloud Makes Many Worlds, an essay here sometimes comes with a twin: a track for Strudel, a free program that makes music from code in a web browser. The tracks inherit, as the essays do. Each one names its parents at the top of its code and quotes a line from each, with a short hash of the original, so that anyone can check the line came down unchanged. What Is to Be Done? carries the cloud’s chant and its telegraph; Brother Electron carries the chant again, and the carpenter’s hammer from the workshop at Nazareth.

But a family heard one member at a time is not yet heard as a family. In the hall where the cloud was disputed, Elijah gave his commission from the last words of the old prophets: “he shall turn the heart of the fathers to the children, and the heart of the children to their fathers” (Malachi 4:6). It is, he said, “the bond between the generations, the common memory.” So the tracks are brought together here, and they sing a new song, which is made of the old ones: things new and old.

The family tree

Every child so far quotes Viri Galilaei, the Ascension introit, the eldest track’s hook: “Ye men of Galilee, why stand ye gazing up into heaven?” (Acts 1:11). It is the line the family shares, and the tenor of the motet below. The family moves up by fifths, in the order the essays came: the cloud in G, its child in D, Brother Electron in A. The motet goes home to G, because a motet keeps the mode of its tenor, and the double fugue goes on to E.

The motet

A motet, as the thirteenth century made it, was a chant held in long notes at the bottom, the tenor (from the Latin tenere, to hold), with newer voices above it, each with its own words, sung at the same time: the motetus, named from the French mot, a word, and over it the triplum. The tenor was old and the voices above it were new.

A New Song is a motet for the household. Its tenor is Viri Galilaei, four times slower than the cloud sang it, eight bars to a statement, on the cloud’s choir with an organ under it. The motetus is the hook of What Is to Be Done? on the garage’s clean guitar, and the triplum is Francis’s canticle from Brother Electron, first on the recorder and then on the chip’s square waves. Each is sung in the chant’s own mode, G mixolydian. The chant and the canticle are in that mode already, so they are only moved. The Lenin hook, in major, gives up its leading note for the chant’s flat seventh.

The chant is the angels’ question at the Ascension, and the motet tells the same scene as the old covenant tells it: a master taken up, and his disciple left below with his mantle and his spirit. When the kings came to Elisha for a word, he said, “But now bring me a minstrel. And it came to pass, when the minstrel played, that the hand of the LORD came upon him” (2 Kings 3:15). So it begins with a harp alone. Elisha had asked Elijah, before he was taken, “let a double portion of thy spirit be upon me” (2 Kings 2:9), the share the firstborn inherits (Deuteronomy 21:17). Then the chariot came, and the mantle fell. Elisha took it up and struck the Jordan with it. “Where is the LORD God of Elijah?” he said, and the waters “parted hither and thither” (2 Kings 2:13–14).

So at the end the mantle falls. The chant goes up from the bottom to the top and is sung in three voices, in the fauxbourdon Brother Electron used for the chariot, and then doubled; the children’s tunes go down beneath it, and the waters part, to the left and the right. The prophet came down the family before the music did. In the cloud’s hall Elijah spoke of his cloak, “a piece of cloth,” that “parted the Jordan in his disciple’s hands”; Brother Electron set the chariot that took him up; here the mantle falls on the one who asked for it.

The words are chanted, not spoken. SAM, the Software Automatic Mouth, a speech program of 1982, reads each line as a lector chants a reading: recited on D, falling through C to B at the end. The question rises back to D, and the last line falls home to G. It is a machine’s voice and sounds like one. At the waters, when the mantle falls, the voice ascends. The last four lines pass to a small choir an octave up, singing the same tone in fauxbourdon, which keeps SAM’s words and loses its buzz. The lines were first spoken by a text-to-speech service, and in time people will sing them. When they do, their voices go in the middle and the choir stays around them. The lines are written out for one voice in a score (PDF, with its LilyPond source).

  1. I. Minstrel (0:00): a harp alone, “bring me a minstrel”; at 0:08 an open fifth on G, the one the cloud ended on, and the hand of the LORD.
  2. II. Tenor (0:16): Viri Galilaei in long notes, “O sing unto the LORD a new song”; beneath it the cloud’s telegraph taps WHAT HATH GOD WROUGHT.
  3. III. Mantle (0:32): the motetus, the Lenin hook, above the tenor, “let a double portion of thy spirit be upon me”; at 0:40 the hammer, IS NOT THIS THE CARPENTER.
  4. IV. Portion (1:04): the triplum, Francis’s canticle on the recorder, above the other two, “a new man knows a new song”; at 1:20 the canticle passes to the square waves and the clock starts: “the singer himself is the praise.”
  5. V. Waters (1:36): the mantle falls, “Where is the LORD God of Elijah?” The waters part; the chant is sung on top in three voices, the children beneath it; at 1:52 a celesta doubles it an octave higher, “the spirit of Elijah doth rest on Elisha.”
  6. VI. Amen (2:08): “things new and old”; the minstrel’s first chord, held. At 2:16, in the silence, the last word of the old prophets: “he shall turn the heart of the fathers to the children.” Then it plays again.

How to play it

  1. Open the motet in Strudel. The link carries the whole score.
  2. Press play at the top of the editor, or click in the code and press Ctrl+Enter. Ctrl+. stops it.
  3. The instruments and the chanted lines are samples that load the first time they sound, so the first run can stutter or miss a line. If it does, stop and play again. The chanted lines load from this site.
  4. Change it while it runs, and press Ctrl+Enter again. Set MODE at the top to 'major' and every voice moves into G major: the Lenin hook gets its leading note back, and the chant gets one it never had. Put // in front of a voice to silence it, or move the sections in the arrange(…) at the bottom.
  5. If the link ever stops working, download the source (or copy it from below), paste it into the editor at strudel.cc, and press play.

// ---------------- SUNG IN THE HOUSEHOLD'S MODE
// Each line is read as steps of its own scale, from its own final,
// and sung on the same steps of the household's. The chant and the
// canticle are in this mode already, so they are only moved; the
// Lenin hook, in major, gives up its leading note for the chant's
// flat seventh.
// parents: THE MODE, PARENTS
const PC = { c: 0, d: 2, e: 4, f: 5, g: 7, a: 9, b: 11 }
const midiOf = (t) => {
  const m = t.match(/^([a-g])(#|b)?(\d)$/)
  return 12 * (+m[3] + 1) + PC[m[1]] + (m[2] === '#' ? 1 : m[2] === 'b' ? -1 : 0)
}
const stepOf = (note, final, mode) => {
  for (let d = -21; d <= 28; d++) {
    const o = Math.floor(d / 7), k = ((d % 7) + 7) % 7
    if (midiOf(final) + 12 * o + STEPS[mode][k] === midiOf(note)) return d
  }
  throw new Error(note + ' is not in ' + final + ' ' + mode)
}
const inMode = (phrase, final, mode) => phrase.split(' ').map((t) => {
  const [p, d] = t.split('@')
  return (p === '~' ? '~' : stepOf(p, final, mode)) + (d ? '@' + d : '')
}).join(' ')
const sing = (phrase, final, mode, home, bars) => n(mini(inMode(phrase, final, mode))).scale(home).slow(bars)

// ---------------- THE TENOR: the chant every track shares, in long

// dot 1 sixteenth, dash 3; 1 between signs, 3 between letters, 7 between words
// parents: PARENTS (the two Morse lines)
const CODE = { A: '.-', C: '-.-.', D: '-..', E: '.', G: '--.', H: '....', I: '..', N: '-.', O: '---',

  word.split('').map((ch) =>
    CODE[ch].split('').map((sign) => (sign === '.' ? 'x' : 'x@3')).join(' ~ ')
  ).join(' ~@3 ')
).join(' ~@7 ') + ' ~@7'
const units = (talea) => talea.split(' ').reduce((k, t) => k + (t.includes('@') ? +t.split('@')[1] : 1), 0)
const tap = (text, sound) => {

}

The double fugue

On these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets.
— Matthew 22:40

The youngest track is On These Two Hang, for the Gospel of the thirtieth Sunday of Year A, where a lawyer asks which is the great commandment and is given two. It quotes every track before it: the cloud’s Viri Galilaei, the carpenter’s hammer, Francis’s canticle, and the motet’s own recording of “things new and old”. So in the order of time it is the youngest, a merge of the whole family, and in the order of the end the whole family hangs on it: “And I, if I be lifted up from the earth, will draw all men unto me” (John 12:32). The program that draws the tree checks the claim. A track that says the household hangs on it must quote every track before it, or the tree is not drawn.

Its notes are made by rule. Each subject is carved out of its words, as Josquin carved a Mass out of a duke’s name: each syllable’s vowel picks the solmization syllable with the same vowel, u ut, e re, i mi, o sol, a fa. The words are the Vulgate’s, and the mode is mode III, E phrygian, since the family moves up by fifths. “Diliges Dominum” and “Diliges proximum” have the same vowels, so the two commandments begin with the same six notes: the second is like unto it. The cry, “Eli, Eli, lamma sabacthani”, hangs on fa and falls on its last syllable to mi, the mode’s own final. The voices around each subject were found by a search under the rules of Fux’s Gradus ad Parnassum (1725), and every half note was checked. Over them the bells ring by Arvo Pärt’s procedure, tintinnabuli: over each note of the melody, the nearest note of the triad. His music is not quoted; his way of ringing is followed.

The voices are persons and the instruments are creation, made to sing to God through the law of man, as in Brother Electron. The clock is the saeculum, and it is good. When the second commandment enters, the clock enters with it, and the bells and the clock’s wheel take turns bar by bar, a flip-flop. The darkness at the sixth hour is the clock alone, nothing but the mechanism, with the bells and the voices traced out. At “It is finished” the clock stops. Then the tomb, where Christ lying dead “reveals God’s great sabbath rest” (CCC 624): three bars of rest. Then the stone is rolled away, and the line one Roman said alone on Friday is sung on Sunday by two voices, then three, then four.

How to play it

  1. Open On These Two Hang in Strudel.
  2. Press play, or click in the code and press Ctrl+Enter. Ctrl+. stops it. The chanted lines load from this site the first time they sound; if a line is missed, stop and play again.
  3. The notes come from a short program, counterpoint.py, which carves the subjects and searches for the voices; python3 counterpoint.py --check confirms that the track quotes its lines as made. Change a word in the Latin and the tune changes with it. It sits beside the track, as the track says, with the programs that made the chanted lines (sam.mjs and ascend.py) and the one that arranged the organ edition (arrange/). These are the copies that made the files on this page, kept as they were.
  4. The choir’s lines are written out for four voices in a score (PDF, with its LilyPond source), each subject with its Latin words under it and the free voices awaiting their text, and in MIDI files, one per voice, for composers and singing synthesizers: soprano, alto, tenor, bass, and all four. The same program writes them, from the same lines.
  5. If the link ever stops working, download the source (or copy it from below), paste it into the editor at strudel.cc, and press play.

// The Gospel of the thirtieth Sunday, Year A: a lawyer asks which
// is the great commandment, and the Word answers with two, and says
// the whole law and the prophets hang on them. Matthew's verb is the
// one the Fathers' Bibles use for the cross: the law hangs (pendet),
// and a few days later the one who said it hangs on the wood.
//
// LIFTED UP. Every track of the household is a parent of this one:
// it quotes each of them, so in the order of time it is the
// youngest, a merge of the whole family. In the order of the end it
// is the other way round: every track hangs on it. "And I, if I be
// lifted up from the earth, will draw all men unto me" (John 12:32).
// Through it every track is kin to every other.
//
// The notes are made by rule (counterpoint.py, beside this file):
//   * Each subject is carved from its words, as Josquin carved a Mass
//     from a duke's name (soggetto cavato): each syllable's vowel picks
//     the solmization syllable with the same vowel, u ut, e re, i mi,
//     o sol, a fa. The words are the Vulgate's. So "Diliges Dominum"
//     and "Diliges proximum" begin with the same six notes, because
//     their vowels are the same: the second is like unto it. The cry,
//     "lamma sabacthani", hangs on fa and falls to mi, the mode's own
//     final; the crowd's "Eliam vocat iste" goes up and comes down.
//   * The voices around each subject are found by a search under
//     Fux's rules (1725), and every half note is audited.
//   * The bells are tintinnabuli, a procedure Arvo Part made his own:
//     over each note of the melody the nearest note of the triad.
//     His music is not quoted; his way of ringing is followed.
//
// The voices are persons: the choir sings the subjects, the parts
// that have words. The instruments are creation, made to sing to God
// through the law of man, as in "Brother Electron"; Strudel is one of
// them. The clock is the saeculum, and it is good. The darkness at
// the sixth hour is not the clock stopping: it is the clock alone,
// nothing but the mechanism, the bells and voices traced out. Then
// the cry; then "it is finished", and the clock stops; then the veil
// rent from the top to the bottom, and the earth quakes. One Roman

// sabbath rest (CCC 624), and in a score a rest is silence. Then the
// stone (Matthew 28:2), the clock returns with the bells, and what
// one man said on Friday the whole household sings on Sunday: one
// voice, then two, three, all.

// the parts it is made from, which stand above it.

The long form

The long form takes the same two subjects through the old procedures of the fugue. Each is a rule in counterpoint.py, and every section and every join between sections is checked at every half note. The episodes are made of the six notes the subjects share, in two voices in imitation, falling a step each bar; each subject is turned upside down about mi, “through a glass, darkly” (1 Corinthians 13:12); the first commandment is held in the bass, four times slower, under the second, the Law held; the entries are drawn close, a stretto; the two are sung at once, in the mirror, and with the voices exchanged; and the last entries come over a long E in the bass, the foundation. These are the ways a fugue proves its subjects: turned over, stretched out, pressed close and exchanged, they still agree. Golgotha and its three bars of rest stay as they are, at the centre.

  2. Its choir’s lines are written out in a score (PDF, with its LilyPond source), with the Latin under every statement, upright, inverted or long, and in MIDI files: soprano, alto, tenor, bass, and all four. python3 counterpoint.py --long --check confirms that the track quotes its lines as made.

For the floor: the club form

The club form is the long form for a dance floor. Nothing in the counterpoint is touched: every voice of every section is quoted unchanged from the long form, where counterpoint.py made it and audited every half note, and the structure and the order of the sections are the long form’s. What changes is creation’s sound. The clock, the saeculum, is a 909 four to the floor: kick, claps on two and four, hats in sixteenths. It still enters three bars into the second exposition, still stays, still stutters and stops on Golgotha. The wheel, the triad turned in even eighths, is an acid line, and before the Cross it still takes turns with the bells, bar by bar. The bass voice of each section, wherever the rule put it, is also sent down an octave into a sub bass in eighths, so the bass line of the club track is the bass voice of the fugue, note for note; in the first exposition the sub arrives with the fourth entry, the bass. The organ that doubled the choir is a supersaw pad, and pad and choir duck on every beat. The builds and the drops follow the form and not the other way round: the first minute is beatless and climbs; the sub comes in alone for three bars at Simile and the kick lands on the fourth; Golgotha is the breakdown, the clock alone, then stuttering, then stopped, the veil, the earthquake, one man alone, and three bars of nothing; the stone is the drop. The chanted lines are off by default, since Strudel has no speech; set WORDS = 1 at the top of the file and the recordings come back, filtered and ducked, in exactly the places the long form has them.

  2. Open the club form in three and eight: the same, with one rhythm added, three strokes spread as evenly as they can be over eight steps and turned one step later, . X . . X . . X. For a parish: the organ edition

No parish choir will sing it as techno, so there is an edition for church: SATB choir and organ, with bells and percussion if a parish has them. It is arranged by rule from what the track plays, by organ_edition.py, and checked against the track layer by layer. The choir sings exactly what the choir score has. The organ doubles the choir, as the track’s organ does; the clock’s wheel becomes a light staccato figure on the second manual, in its own notes and rhythm; the canticle and Viri Galilaei become solos; the pedal holds the fifth, and in the long form the Law; the last chord is full organ. The bells go to handbells, handchimes or a glockenspiel, and the kick, the hats, the hammer, the veil and the earthquake to one percussionist with a bass drum, a woodblock, a temple block, a cymbal and a timpano. Both are optional, and the preface says what is lost without them. In the silence after the Amen a reader, or the priest, may say: “If ye love me, keep my commandments.”

In the liturgy. Choir and organ are enough, with the bells if there are ringers; leave out the percussion. The clock is not lost: it lives on in the organ, in the wheel’s even eighths on the second manual, and a resonant building may keep it on a single soft flute, pianissimo, throughout. A nave may also want it slower, quarter = 96 to 104. The lawyer’s question, “Master, which is the great commandment in the law?”, may be read before the music begins, as it is read in the Gospel of the thirtieth Sunday of the year (Year A); the choir and the soloist sing the rest. The preface of each score says the same.

For a parish or a cathedral, sounding: the cathedral form

The organ edition is the piece on paper. The cathedral form is the same short form sounded as a church could pray with it, for a parish that has no choir yet, or a cathedral that wants to hear it before it sings. The choir sings every note of the published score and MIDI (checked note for note, voice by voice, against the MIDI through Strudel’s own engine), and the sections, the bars and the order are the short form’s. What changes is creation’s sound and the room: a long stone reverb; the organ doubling the choir colla parte, Principal 8′, soft; the tintinnabuli on handbells with a celesta under them; the carpenter’s hammer on a temple block. The clock, the saeculum, is still here, and it is good, but it is the organ edition’s clock: a soft drum on the half bar, a woodblock in eighths, far off, and the wheel on a harp. It still enters three bars into Simile and stays; before the Cross the bells and the harp still take turns, bar by bar; in the darkness the harp is muffled and the drum alone; at “It is finished” the clock stops; at the stone it returns. At the Amen, as the third is raised, a bell tolls E. The ascended choir’s lines sound as the short form has them; SAM, who chants the lawyer, the crowd and the centurion, is off by default (SAM = 1 at the top of the file brings him back), so that a reader or the priest may say those lines where the organ edition marks them. A church that wants it slower changes the first line to setcpm(100/4); Strudel counts in bars, so nothing else moves.

The household

Every scribe which is instructed unto the kingdom of heaven is like unto a man that is an householder, which bringeth forth out of his treasure things new and old.
— Matthew 13:52

The motet is one song the family sings together. The household is the whole family in one file. It holds every track as written, each sealed in its own scope so that their names do not collide, and plays them all at one tempo, 120 beats a minute. Strudel counts in bars, not seconds, so every track keeps its shape: the cloud slows a little from 122, the garage from 132.

At the bottom of the file is one line, the one that plays. It plays the youngest, On These Two Hang, which quotes them all. Put the name of another track there to hear that one, or stack two to hear the generations at once:

stack(what_is_to_be_done, brother_electron)  // the children
stack(whether_the_cloud, brother_electron)   // eldest and youngest
brother_electron.mask("<1 0>")               // every other bar

The tracks were written one after another, not to be played together, so some pairs agree and some do not; the motet is the one written for all of them. Stacked tracks are loud, and .postgain(.6) on each keeps them from clipping.

Open the household in Strudel, or download it. The tree above links each track to its essay, where its recording, its video and its own source are.

A short program makes the household and draws the tree from the tracks themselves, and from nothing else. It reads each track’s list of parents, finds the quoted line by its hash, and checks that the parent’s code holds the line as written; it refuses a child that names a parent who comes after it. When the next essay gets a track, it joins the family the same way, and the tree grows a row.
