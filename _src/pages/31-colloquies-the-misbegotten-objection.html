The Misbegotten Objection

Aristotle, Thomas Aquinas, and Edith, who is writing a doctorate on women in their work and loves them both. She has the texts. They have to argue.

Richard Verver · October 2026

A reading room, late. Edith has two books open and a third closed under her hand. The two men have been sitting across from her for some time, as if they had always been there. Neither of them has the look of someone who has seen God. They have the look of men who are prepared to argue.

Edith. I should say first that I love you both. I want that on the record before I say the rest, because the rest is going to sound like an indictment, and it is one.

Aristotle. Begin with the indictment. The affection can come after, if there is anything left of it.

Edith. Very well. Generation of Animals, book two. The female is, as it were, a mutilated male. πεπηρωμένον. The female contributes the matter and the male the form; the male is the active principle and the female the passive; and when generation goes well it produces a male, and when something interferes, a female. Politics, book one: the male is by nature superior, the female inferior, the one rules and the other is ruled, and this holds of all mankind. And then the sentence I have had on the wall above my desk for three years, because I cannot get past it. The deliberative faculty is present in the woman, but it is ἄκυρον. Without authority. She can reason, but her reasoning does not get to count.

Aristotle. You have quoted me correctly. Go on.

Edith. Brother Thomas. Summa, first part, question ninety-two. Woman is defective and misbegotten. Deficiens et occasionatum. The active power in the male seed tends to produce a perfect likeness of itself, which is male, and the production of a woman comes from a defect in that power, or from an indisposition of the matter, or from some external influence, such as a south wind, which is moist. And then, two questions on, the image of God is in both, but, you add, in a secondary way the image is found in man and not in woman, because man is the beginning and end of woman as God is the beginning and end of creation. And in the Supplement, on orders: since it is not possible in the female sex to signify eminence of degree, for a woman is in a state of subjection, she cannot receive the sacrament. And in the second part, on whether a woman may teach publicly: no, because of her subjection, and lest men’s minds be enticed to lust.

Thomas. Also correctly quoted. Though you have left out the sentences that come after each of them, and I think you know that.

Edith. I do know that, and I am coming to them, because they are the heart of my thesis. Here is the indictment. It is not that you were sexists. Everyone in Athens and everyone in Paris believed what you believed about women, and most of them believed it more crudely. It is that you were the two most systematic minds in the history of the West, and you took a piece of bad biology and built it into the foundations of a metaphysics, a politics, and a theology, so carefully and so well that when the biology collapsed, the building stood. That is the harm. Not the error. The architecture. The premise was refuted; Harvey in the seventeenth century, the ovum in the nineteenth, the chromosomes in the twentieth, and it turned out that the woman contributes exactly as much form as the man and that there is no passive principle anywhere in the business. And nothing that had been built on the premise fell down. The conclusions had been so beautifully fitted to the rest of the system that they found new reasons to go on standing. In the Church above all. The Church kept your conclusions and quietly replaced your arguments, and no one was ever asked to say out loud that the old arguments had been false.

Aristotle. Stop there. I want to take the biology first, because it is mine, and because you have been fair to it and I intend to be fairer. I cut open more animals than any man before me. I looked at hens’ eggs on every day of the twenty-one and wrote down what I saw. I was wrong about the female contribution. I thought the catamenia were the matter and that the male semen set it, as rennet sets milk, and I was wrong, and if you had shown me the ovum under your glass I would have thrown out the chapter and started again, and I would have been glad to, because the chapter was never the point. The method was the point. Begin from what appears. Collect what the reputable say. Save as much of the appearances as you can. And when the appearances change, change.

Edith. But you did not say “the appearances suggest.” You said “by nature.” Nature is the strongest word you have.

Aristotle. Nature is what happens always or for the most part, and I inferred it from what I observed. If what you observe is different, then nature is different from what I said, and the word stays and the content goes. I will say this plainly, because you deserve it. The sentence about the deliberative faculty was not a deduction. It was an observation of Athenian women, who were kept indoors, denied letters, married at fourteen to men of thirty, and never once in their lives asked to deliberate about anything that would be acted on. I observed that their deliberation lacked authority. It did. I made the error that an observer always makes, which is to mistake the condition of the thing observed for its nature. I have now observed you for an hour. Your deliberation has authority. So either my sentence was false or you are not a woman, and I know which.

Edith. That is more than I expected from you.

Aristotle. It is less than you should expect from anyone who says he follows the appearances. The ones who should be ashamed are the ones who kept the sentence after they had the observation.

Thomas. May I take mine? Because I accept the charge in a form you have not quite stated, and I want to state it exactly. You read “deficiens et occasionatum” as my view. It is not my view. It is the objection. The article asks whether woman should have been made in the first production of things, and the first objection says no, because the Philosopher says the female is a mutilated male, and nothing mutilated should have been in the first creation. And I answer: as regards the particular nature, yes, the Philosopher’s biology says what it says. But as regards the universal nature, woman is not misbegotten but intended, by nature and by God, for the work of generation. The woman is not a mistake. She is willed. I was contradicting Aristotle in the only register I had, which was his own.

Edith. You were contradicting him by conceding him. You let the biology stand in the individual case and overruled it only at the level of God’s intention. That is precisely the architecture I mean. You saved the woman as a species and left her defective as a person.

Thomas. Yes. That is just. I accepted the natural philosophy of my time because it was the best there was, and I built on it, as I built on his physics and his astronomy, and his physics and his astronomy are also gone. No one holds me to the crystalline spheres. But you are right that they hold me to this, and you are right about why. The spheres did not touch anyone’s standing. This did. And I will say what you want me to say, because it is true and because I would have said it in Paris if anyone had brought me the ovum. An argument from a false premise proves nothing. Where I argued from the Philosopher’s generation, the argument is void, and whatever rests on it alone must be examined again from the beginning. I said as much, in a different place, about the faith: that to defend a truth with a bad argument gives unbelievers occasion to laugh and does the truth harm. I did not think I was doing it. I was.

Edith. Then do the examination. Here. The ordination argument.

Thomas. The argument I gave in the commentary on the Sentences, which the Supplement copied after my death, rests on signification: a sacrament signifies, and orders signifies eminence, and a woman in a state of subjection cannot signify eminence. Two premises. That orders signifies eminence I would still hold. That woman is in a state of subjection, by nature, I took from the Philosopher, and from a reading of Genesis that leaned on the Philosopher. If subjection by nature falls, that argument falls with it. It has fallen. I notice that the Church, when she spoke to this question in your century, did not use my argument. She argued from what Christ did and from what she has always done, and she said she did not consider herself authorised to do otherwise. Whether that argument is good is not what you asked me. You asked me whether mine was. It was not, and the Church was right to set it down, and she should have said why.

Edith. That is the thing no one says. She should have said why.

Thomas. A conclusion can be true when an argument for it is bad. But a conclusion that keeps its bad argument in the cellar, unexamined, becomes a prejudice, and prejudice is the form a dead argument takes when no one has buried it. If the Church holds a thing on Christ’s authority she should say so and stop borrowing from my biology. If she holds a thing on my biology, she should let it go, because the biology is gone.

Aristotle. I want to object to Thomas, since no one else here will. You have given up too much. You have said subjection by nature falls. Very well; but you have said nothing about what stands in its place, and I will not let the question be answered by a wave of the hand. If the woman and the man each contribute form, then they are, in that respect, equals; and between equals, I said, rule is by turns, as among citizens, and not by nature. That is a political consequence and it is mine and I will own it. But it does not follow that every difference has vanished, and your century seems to me to have replaced one dogma with its opposite, which is not science but mood. Tell me what you observe.

Edith. I observe that women deliberate with authority when they are permitted to, and that in every field where the permission was granted the results followed within a generation, and that this has now been tried on every continent, and that the one institution in the West that has not run the experiment is the one that keeps your books in its curriculum.

Aristotle. Then that is a datum, and the institution should look at it with the same eyes it uses for everything else. I did not say women should not be observed. I said I had observed them, and I had observed them in a cage.

Thomas. And I would add the thing that was mine to add, and that I did say, and that you are entitled to throw at me because I did not follow it far enough. The image of God is in the mind. The mind has no sex. I wrote that the image is in both man and woman as regards the principal thing, the intellectual nature, and that the secondary sense, the one you quoted, concerns only a certain order of origin. I should have seen that the secondary sense would be read as the primary by every man who wanted it to be, and that the sentence about origin would be used to keep the image from counting. It is a kind of negligence to write a true thing in such a way that it will be used for a false one.

Edith. You also said, in the same part, that the woman was not made from the man’s head, to rule him, nor from his feet, to be trodden on, but from his side, as a companion. I have always loved that. And I have always noticed that it did not stop you writing the Supplement.

Thomas. No. It did not. I held a true thing and a false thing in the same mind and the false thing was in the law, where it did the damage, and the true thing was in the commentary, where it was admired. That is how it goes. That is, I suspect, your whole thesis, and I think you are right.

Edith. Then what is to be done? I do not mean what should be felt. I mean what is to be done, by the people who still read you, in the institution that still reads you.

Aristotle. I will go first because my answer is shorter. Finish the inquiry I started and could not finish. Take every sentence in which I said “by nature” of women and ask whether it was a deduction or an observation, and if it was an observation, ask whether the thing observed was a nature or a condition. Most of them will turn out to be the condition. Publish the list. Do not apologise for me and do not excuse me; I did not ask for either and I would despise both. Correct me, which is what I asked of everyone, and which almost no one did for two thousand years because it was easier to quote me.

Thomas. Mine is longer, because I made the mess longer. Four things.

First, tell the truth about the texts. Do not smooth them. The Church has spent a century explaining that I did not quite mean the sentences I wrote, and this has protected me and injured her. Read the objection and the reply and say which is which, and say that the reply leaned on the objection’s biology, and say that the biology is false. The faithful can bear it. I could.

Second, make the distinction I failed to make in practice, between what rests on revelation and what rests on nature as I knew it. Everything in the second category is open, and must be re-derived from the nature you now know, by my own method, which is to begin again from the truth. Do not keep a conclusion because it is old. Keep it if it is true, and know why.

Third, where the Church has kept a conclusion and changed its reason, make her say the new reason out loud, and if the new reason is Christ’s will, let her say that it is Christ’s will and not the south wind. A people cannot assent to an argument they are not shown. And if she cannot give a reason for a thing beyond “it has always been so,” then she must at least know that “it has always been so” was, for most of that time, my biology wearing a cassock, and she must decide, honestly, whether anything else is underneath.

Fourth, and this is the one I care about, because it is justice and justice was always what I cared about most: render to each what is due. That is a habit, not an opinion, and it is practised in rooms. Who sits in the tribunal. Who is asked. Whose deliberation is allowed to have authority over a thing that will be acted on. The Philosopher’s word was ἄκυρον, without authority. The cure for ἄκυρον is not a document. It is to give the authority, in the actual room, and see what happens, which is what your century did everywhere but one place.

Edith. And the one place?

Thomas. Is the place where I did the most damage, and so it is the place where I would most like to be corrected. I cannot tell you what Christ wills about the altar. I can tell you that I never knew either, and that I wrote as if I did, from premises that were not his.

Edith. You know what my examiners will say. That I have put words in your mouths.

Aristotle. You have put arguments in our mouths. That is what we were for. A man who leaves behind a method has given everyone who comes after him the right to use it against him, and the ones who use it are his students, and the ones who only quote him are not.

Thomas. And you have done the thing I asked for on the first page of the Summa, which is to be the beginner for whom the thing is written, and to ask the question as if it had not been answered. It has not been answered. Ask it.

Edith. I did say I loved you both.

Thomas. You said it before the indictment. Say it after.

Edith. After. More, if anything. It is easier to love a man who has been wrong in the open than one who has been right behind a door.

Aristotle. Then write it down. That was an observation, and observations are where we begin.

She opens the third book. It is blank. They are gone, or they were never there, and the question is still on the table, which is where it belongs.
