Homeschool curriculum

The Benedict Year

A 36-week homeschool year for ages 10 to 13. It walks the map of the disciplines in order, from reading and number up to worship, with Mr. Benedict’s school from Trenton Lee Stewart’s novels as its method and Mr. Curtain’s Institute as its warning.

The essays’ map says it is a map, not a course. This is one course through it, and any order that keeps the arrows would do as well. Parents should start with the page for parents: the essays were written for adults, and some need a decision before they are read aloud.

How the year works

The year runs 36 weeks in twelve units of three weeks, one for each discipline on the map. They come in the map’s own order, so nothing is studied before what it draws on. The units are a lens: the family’s own courses carry the disciplines all year (see the strands), and each unit gathers what its discipline draws from them.

Mr. Benedict’s method is how it teaches. He did not recruit the children who knew the most answers. He set puzzles that could only be solved by thinking, rewarded the honest answer over the clever one, and sent his children out as a team whose gifts covered each other’s weaknesses. So every week here opens with a puzzle, ends with a test of the Benedict kind, and is done together.

Montessori and the atrium shape the day. Each morning is a long, uninterrupted work period, and the year’s prayer runs through an atrium of the Catechesis of the Good Shepherd. Neither keeps score, and neither does Mr. Benedict: see Montessori and the atrium.

L.I.V.E. is the warning. Mr. Curtain’s Learning Institute for the Very Enlightened trains children to repeat what they are given, rewards them for it, and hides its real messages inside its lessons. Each unit names the Institute habit its discipline guards against, and the child practises spotting it.

The map gives each unit its rule. Every discipline on the map gives one kind of answer: what is read, computed, found, made, dated, understood, said by analogy, taught, judged, supplied, enacted, witnessed. A unit is three weeks spent learning to tell that kind of answer from the others.

A week

DayThe presentation that opens the morning
MondayThe puzzle: the opening problems of the week’s mathematics, or the unit’s own puzzle, tried before any explanation
TuesdayThe book: the Benedict Society novel and the week’s questions
WednesdayThe discipline: the unit’s material, or something to make
ThursdayThe essay: in essay weeks, read aloud and talked over, or in a gap unit the child’s own; otherwise a project
FridayThe test: a question hidden in a question, an honesty question, a team problem, and spotting the Whisperer; observed, never marked

After the presentation, the rest of the morning is the child’s, at the shelf. Classes sit where their schedules fall, even when one cuts into the morning, and the rest of the work period is kept whole around them. Afternoons hold the longer courses, something to make, and going out. The atrium meets once a week, for two hours or more.

A unit

  • Week 1, the kind of answer. What this discipline answers, and how it knows. Mostly puzzles and things to do.
  • Week 2, the essay. The parent reads the essay first, then reads it aloud or retells it. Where the map has a gap, the child writes into it: a short essay of their own in a borrowed voice, as the site’s essays are written.
  • Week 3, the case. One worked case that shows the unit’s rule doing its work, then a short piece of writing.

The Benedict Year is offered for families to use and adapt. It draws on The Mysterious Benedict Society novels by Trenton Lee Stewart (Little, Brown) but is not connected with the author or his publishers; buy or borrow the books. The essays it uses are personal essays, not legal advice and not teaching on behalf of any tribunal, diocese or society I serve.
