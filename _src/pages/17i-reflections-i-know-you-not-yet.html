Biblical reflections

Reflection · a layman’s reading, revisited

I Know You Not (Yet)

The ten virgins a second time, read from the bridal chamber, as a fourth voice over the three Sundays of Matthew 25. Who the ten are, why the bride is missing, the rival wives of the Old Testament, what it means to be known, where the chamber is, and a kingdom where no one is a rival. Food for thought, not teaching; the liberties are marked where they are taken.

Richard Verver · November 2026

The Church gives the last three Sundays of her year to one chapter of Matthew, the twenty-fifth: the ten virgins, the talents, the sheep and the goats. A door, a darkness, a throne. I have written on each of them in turn, and I thought I had finished. But a hymn is not finished when the lower voices have sung it through. On the last verse there is sometimes one more voice, a descant, that comes in above the others. It brings no new tune. It sings over the top of what is already there, and you hear a harmony that was in the hymn all along.

This is that voice, or an attempt at it. It goes back to the first of the three parables, because I walked past the most obvious thing in it, the way a man walks past the house where the party is. I kept to the lamps, the oil and the door. I read I know you not as a sentence of dismissal, and as Peter’s words in the courtyard, and as the first words of an acquaintance, the place where a conversation could begin. I still think all of that. But it is a wedding. The kingdom of heaven, Our Lord says, is like a wedding at night: torches coming down a road, the smell of burning oil, girls’ feet in the dust, and at the end of the road a house, and in the house a room with a door. All three parables were told at one sitting, on the Mount of Olives, by a man who had two days to live, and the first of them says that the end of the world will be like that. I want to go back into the chapter by that door.

The Bible is not shy about that room, and I will not be either; but I will try to speak of it as the Bible does, plainly and without leering. The Song of Songs is in the canon. So is the verb I want to talk about.

Ten virgins, and no bride

Some modern translations call them bridesmaids, and the lectionary read at Mass in this country is one of them. Matthew does not. He calls them parthenoi, virgins, and the Authorized Version keeps the word. Matthew uses it of only one other woman in his Gospel, and she is a betrothed girl: Behold, a virgin shall be with child, said of Mary, who was espoused to Joseph, before they came together. The only other virgin in Matthew is a bride who has not yet gone to her husband’s house.

And look for the bride in the parable. She is not there. The bridegroom is named four times; the bride never. The copyists noticed. Some early copies, Greek, Latin and Syriac, add three words to the first verse, so that the virgins go out to meet the bridegroom and the bride; the Vulgate has them, and so the Douay does too. The critical text and the Authorized Version leave her out. It has been argued that she was dropped on purpose, because a bride waiting somewhere else sat badly with Christ coming to fetch his Church. Metzger, writing for the editors of the critical text, doubts that copyists were so alert to the allegory, and thinks it likelier that she was added, by scribes who pictured the bridegroom bringing his bride home. Whichever way it went, the scribes felt the question, and I feel it too. As Matthew most likely wrote it, it is a wedding, and there is no bride. Where is she?

Here is the liberty, marked as mine. I think no bride is named because the ten are the bride. They are not the girl’s friends, holding lamps for her. They are the betrothed: ten girls on a dark road, each with her own small flame, each waiting for the same man to come and take her home. That is not how weddings are done among us, and it was not the usual way in Galilee either. But it is a way the Bible knows very well. Israel’s fathers had more than one wife. Jacob married two sisters in one week. A king had a house of women, and the Psalm of the royal wedding says that the virgins who follow the queen shall be brought unto thee, into the king’s palace, with gladness. One bridegroom and many brides is not a picture foreign to Scripture. It is one of its oldest, and one of its most painful.

I should say at once who these virgins are, so that no one thinks I have wandered off into anthropology. St Paul tells the Corinthians, men and women together, I have espoused you to one husband, that I may present you as a chaste virgin to Christ. The Apocalypse calls the men who follow the Lamb virgins. In the language of Scripture, every soul that waits for Christ is betrothed to him, the bishop and the bricklayer as much as the nun. The ten on the road are us.

Rival wives

The Old Testament tells the truth about that picture. Hebrew has a word for a second wife, and the word is tsarah: rival. It comes from a verb that means to vex, to treat as an enemy, and it is spelled the same as the word for distress. Hannah had one. Year by year the household went up to Shiloh for the sacrifice, and at the meal Peninnah had sons and daughters to be served and Hannah had none, and Peninnah saw to it that she felt it; therefore she wept, and did not eat. The Authorized Version calls Peninnah her adversary. The Hebrew is simply her rival. When Hannah prays at last, she prays so hard that only her lips move, and the priest takes her for a drunk. The Law of Moses forbade a man to marry his wife’s sister while the first one lived, and it gave the reason in one word: to vex her. The verb means to make her a rival. Sister wives were forbidden because a sister would become a tsarah.

Jacob had done it anyway, and Genesis does not spare us the result. Leah was tender eyed; but Rachel was beautiful. The LORD saw that Leah was hated. She bears a son and names him for her hope: now therefore my husband will love me. She bears a third and tries again: Now this time will my husband be joined unto me. She names her children the way a prisoner scratches days on a wall. And all the while Rachel envied her sister. At the worst of it Rachel asks Leah for some mandrakes her boy has found in the field, and Leah answers with the sentence of every rival wife who ever lived: Is it a small matter that thou hast taken my husband? They strike a bargain, and that evening Leah goes out to meet Jacob coming in from the field and tells him, I have hired thee. The bridegroom has become a thing in short supply, to be hired by the night.

Sarah gave her maid to Abraham, and when the maid conceived, there were two women in one tent and room for neither. In the end Hagar was put out of doors at dawn with bread and a bottle of water, and she went into the wilderness, and the water was spent in the bottle. A woman shut out of the house with a vessel that has run dry: I did not invent the picture. But see what happens to her outside. God meets her there, and opens her eyes, and she sees a well. Years before, on her first flight, she had given God a name, the first person in Scripture to do so: Thou God seest me. When I wrote on the talents I said of the servant cast into the darkness outside that he was thrown towards his Lord. Hagar found it so first. The one shut out of the house is the one who is seen.

Now go back to the parable and listen to the five with flasks. Not so; lest there be not enough for us and you: but go ye rather to them that sell, and buy for yourselves. I said in the first reflection that this was prudence talking, and I still say it is not malice. But I can hear another voice in it now. It is the voice of the rival wife. There is one bridegroom, and he is late, and the night is long, and there is not enough of him to go round. Go and buy your own, as Leah bought her night from Rachel. This is not wickedness. It is arithmetic. A man is finite; he cannot give the whole of himself to two women at once, and every wife in Jacob’s tent knew it. The rivalry of the tsarah is what love looks like when it has to be divided. It is mammon’s arithmetic too. In The Unprofitable Servant I read the lord of the next parable, in the letter of the story, as mammon, a man who reaps where he did not sow. In Jacob’s tent the bridegroom himself has become mammon’s coin, paid for in mandrakes and hired by the night. Oil can be bought at midnight. This Bridegroom cannot.

I have now heard that arithmetic in all three parables. The wise count their oil, and there is not enough. The lord of the talents counts his silver, and his rule is that from him that hath not shall be taken away even that which he hath; I said of that proverb that it is not a law of grace, it is how money works. And the goats, as I read them, are careful people with sound budgets, who had counted what the stranger would cost. Oil, silver, bread: there is only so much, and what you have, I have not. That is true of oil. The whole question of the chapter is whether it is true of the Bridegroom.

The reasoning is short enough to set down. A gift of the whole self leaves nothing over. A man who has given the whole of himself to one woman has nothing whole left to give a second; so the second must take from the first, or go without. Give a finite person wholly to two, and you have not doubled the love. You have made rivals. It is the oldest observation in the Bible, and the Law packed it into a single verb.

To know

And they were both naked, the man and his wife, and were not ashamed. That is the last verse before the serpent. The first thing the stolen knowledge does is to set them sewing leaves and hiding among the trees, so as not to be seen. And then, outside the garden, the Bible uses its own word for knowing: And Adam knew Eve his wife; and she conceived. In Hebrew the verb is yada, and it is the ordinary verb for knowing anything at all: a road, a face, the difference between good and evil. It is also the Bible’s word for what happens in the bridal chamber. Scripture has other ways of saying it, but for the first man and woman it chose the plain word for knowing, and it does not think it is being coy. To know someone, in the Bible’s language, is to have been given to them and to have received them, body and all. To be known is to be seen as you are, and not sent away.

I must be honest about the Greek. When the bridegroom says I know you not, Matthew’s verb is oida, the plain word for knowing, the same one Peter uses by the fire; it is not ginosko, the verb the Greek Old Testament uses for Adam and Eve. So the double sense I am about to lean on is not printed on the page of Matthew. But the man who spoke the sentence spoke it in a Semitic tongue, to Jews, about a wedding, and in that tongue the one verb served for both.

Now hear it again, as a betrothed girl would hear it at the door of the chamber. I know you not. Of course he does not. A betrothed girl is not yet known. That is what it means to be betrothed. When the angel came to the most famous betrothed virgin in the Gospels, she answered him with almost the same words: How shall this be, seeing I know not a man? The not-knowing of a virgin is not a rejection. It is the state of one who has been promised and not yet taken home.

And notice what the bridegroom does not say. Twice elsewhere in Matthew the Lord gives sentence at the last day, and both times he says depart. At the end of the Sermon on the Mount: I never knew you: depart from me. At the end of this same speech, to the goats: Depart from me, ye cursed. Here, and only here, there is no never and no depart. There is the door, and the voice, and the present tense. I know you not.

So here is the second liberty, and it is the one in the title. I hear a word under the sentence that the text does not print: yet. I know you not yet.

I did not pluck the word out of the air. There is another wedding in the Gospels, and Our Lord says it there. At Cana the wine runs out; at a wedding, of all places, there is not enough. His mother does not say, go ye to them that sell. She says to him, They have no wine. And he answers her with what sounds like a shut door: Woman, what have I to do with thee? mine hour is not yet come. Not yet. She does not argue, and she does not go away. She turns to the servants: Whatsoever he saith unto you, do it. And then there are six stone jars, and more than a hundred gallons of wine, and the governor of the feast is saying to the bridegroom, thou hast kept the good wine until now. A not yet from this man, at a wedding, to someone asking on behalf of others, is not a no.

St Paul says something like it of all of us, in the most famous chapter he wrote: For now we see through a glass, darkly; but then face to face: now I know in part; but then shall I know even as also I am known. God knows us already, to the last hair; that is not what is missing. What is missing is the knowing of the chamber, face to face, the union that waits on the wedding. In that sense every one of us is a virgin outside the door.

I do not say that this unsays the warning. The door is shut; Matthew says Watch therefore, and he means it, and a sentence spoken through a shut door at midnight is a fearful thing to hear. I say only that the sentence the bridegroom chose is the one a bridegroom would say to a bride, and not the one a judge says to a criminal. Matthew had the judge’s sentence ready to hand, twice. He did not use it here.

The door

They that were ready went in with him to the marriage: and the door was shut. Read the last four words slowly. Matthew does not say who shut it. The verb is passive, and the hand on the door is not named. It may have been a servant’s. It may have been the bridegroom’s. A reader who has just heard the five with flasks say go ye rather to them that sell may be forgiven for wondering whether it was theirs.

There is another door at night in the Bible, and another knock, and it is in the Song of Songs. There the one outside is the bridegroom, with the dew in his hair. I sleep, but my heart waketh: it is the voice of my beloved that knocketh, saying, Open to me, my sister, my love, my dove, my undefiled. All ten virgins slept, and here the bride is sleeping too. She is slow. She has put off her coat; she has washed her feet and does not want to soil them. By the time she rises, her fingers wet with myrrh on the handles of the lock, my beloved had withdrawn himself, and was gone. She runs out into the city after him. I sought him, but I could not find him; I called him, but he gave me no answer. The watchmen find her, and beat her, and take away her veil. It is the parable turned inside out: in the Song the bride is inside and the bridegroom shut out, and she is the one who was not ready.

And the Song does not end there. That night is in the fifth chapter of eight. She goes on seeking him. The daughters of Jerusalem ask her, Whither is thy beloved gone?, and she knows the answer: My beloved is gone down into his garden. In the Song, the shut door is one night in the story of a love. It is not the last page.

There is one more door, in a king’s house this time. In the book of Esther, the virgins of a whole empire are gathered for one king. Each is prepared for a year, six months with oil of myrrh, and then goes in to him once; after that she came in unto the king no more, except the king delighted in her, and that she were called by name. The law of that palace was a shut door. Whoever went in to the king without being called was put to death, unless he held out the golden sceptre. Persia’s harem is not God’s kingdom, and I would not have anyone think the Bible admires it. But it is the stage on which the Bible sets one of its bravest scenes. Esther, who had been chosen and had a place, did not use it to keep the king for herself. Her people were to be destroyed, and she went to the shut door uncalled: if I perish, I perish. And when the king saw her standing in the court, she obtained favour in his sight: and the king held out to Esther the golden sceptre. A woman outside a door she had no right to open, asking for others. And it opened.

The chamber is a cross

What is behind the door? The parable does not take us in. But the Gospel does, two days later, and it is not the room we expected.

When I wrote on the sheep and the goats I said that the throne of his glory is a cross. I have to say now that the bridal chamber is the same place. This King has only the one piece of furniture.

Go back to the first wedding. And the LORD God caused a deep sleep to fall upon Adam, and he slept; and while he slept his side was opened, and the woman was made from what was taken out of it, and brought to him. He woke and said, This is now bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh. Then comes the sentence Our Lord quotes when they ask him about divorce, and they shall be one flesh; and a page later, Adam knew Eve his wife. A sleep, an opened side, a bride, a knowing.

St Paul read that page as a prophecy. He quotes the verse about one flesh and adds: This is a great mystery: but I speak concerning Christ and the church. And he says how this husband loved his bride: he gave himself for it.

So the Fathers read St John’s Passion with Genesis open beside it. He bowed his head, and gave up the ghost. The soldiers come, and one of them with a spear pierced his side, and forthwith came there out blood and water. St Augustine says that this second Adam “bowed His head and fell asleep on the cross”, so that a spouse might be formed for him from what flowed out of the sleeper’s side; and from that side, he says, the sacraments of the Church flowed, the blood for the remission of sins and the water for the font and the cup. All ten virgins slept; I noticed it in the first reflection and could make nothing of it. Here is the Bridegroom’s sleep. It is on the wood, and while he sleeps the bride is taken from his side.

Augustine noticed one thing more. His Latin Bible did not say that the soldier pierced the side. It said that he opened it, and Augustine thought the word was chosen with care, so that we should see a gate of life thrown open there. A door was shut in the parable. Two days later a door was opened, and it was in the Bridegroom’s body, and it has not been shut since. The risen Lord showed it to Thomas, and told him to put in his hand.

So if I ask how the Bridegroom comes to know the betrothed, body and all, this is where the Gospels send me. As God he knew us before we were made. But the knowing of a bridegroom is in the flesh, and he took flesh to have it. In the third parable he tells us what he knew there: I was an hungred. I was thirsty. Naked. At Cana he made more than a hundred gallons of wine for a village wedding; on the cross he said I thirst, and they gave him vinegar. Adam and his wife were naked and not ashamed; the second Adam was stripped by soldiers, and the Epistle to the Hebrews says that he, for the joy that was set before him endured the cross, despising the shame. The lord of the talents had a joy to offer his servants too, and I said what it came to: more silver to mind. This is the other Lord’s joy. It is a wedding, and he went to it naked.

And the night before, at table, he had said what a bridegroom says: This is my body which is given for you. Given, and received: that is what yada means. We are not yet known as a bride is known. But the betrothed has been given a pledge to hold in the meantime, and the pledge is his flesh. Before Communion the priest holds it up and says, Blessed are those called to the supper of the Lamb; and the Apocalypse, where the words come from, says which supper it is: the marriage supper of the Lamb.

This, St John says, is the glory. At Cana he manifested forth his glory, at a wedding, with the hour not yet come. When the hour comes, he names it: The hour is come, that the Son of man should be glorified, and he is speaking of a grain of wheat that falls into the ground and dies. I quoted that verse for the servant who buried his talent, and again for the King upon his throne. It belongs here most of all. Glory, I said then, is goodness seen. The glory of a bridegroom is to give himself whole, and there is one place where we have seen that done.

A grain in the ground is one of the oldest pictures there is of a man and a woman, and the ancients mostly drew it the same way: he is the sower, and she is the field. Greek had one word, sperma, for what the farmer scatters and what the husband gives. Plato has lovers sowing the womb like ploughed land. An Athenian father betrothed his daughter with a formula: he gave her “for the ploughing of legitimate children.” Aristotle made a science of it. The man gives the form and the motion; the woman only the stuff, which his seed sets as rennet sets milk. He acts, and she is acted upon. In The Misbegotten Objection I let the Philosopher answer for that, and he owns the error handsomely.

Now watch what the Bridegroom does with that picture when his hour comes. He does not take the sower’s part. He takes the seed’s. Except a corn of wheat fall into the ground and die, it abideth alone: but if it die, it bringeth forth much fruit. In the parables he is the Sower. In his hour the Sower is sown. He goes down into the dark of the ground and lies there, in the place the old world gave to the woman and called passive. And nothing in it is passive. No man taketh it from me, but I lay it down of myself. He went down there by his own consent, and no deed of his was ever more his own.

Nor was she ever the field. The old picture went wrong at the root, because no one simply receives and no one simply gives. Each is always toward someone. The womb takes in the seed and shelters it; nine months on, toward the child, it is the giver, and pours a life into the world in the most violent self-giving in nature. The man who gives himself in love stands with open hands before the Author of a life he could not make. There is no active sex and no passive one. There are two phasings of one pattern, set a step apart, and the icon of marriage is written by the offset, as a harmony is written by the interval between two voices, which neither can sing alone. When the two are one flesh, it is the union, and neither of them singly, that is the one source of the child’s body: far off, and in a creature’s way, as the Father and the Son are one source of the Spirit, and not two; though there it is by one power, and here by the union of two. And the child’s soul is no harvest of theirs at all. God creates each one himself, with no one in between.

And the offset has a floor and a ceiling. Beneath it lies a clay the sexes share. Carbon has no sex; his and hers were forged in stars that died before the sun was born. That is why the first wedding song praises not her difference but their likeness: bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh. Above it stands one bridal place before God, where they neither marry, nor are given in marriage. And all the way through, the one shape returns at every scale, like itself and never the same. The bride receives, and bears. The grain is received by the ground, and bears much fruit. All of it is an image of the Son, who receives all that he has from the Father and with him gives. But do not set God at the top of the pattern, as its largest turn. He is not a turn of it at all. He is the one the whole pattern is of. The Fourth Lateran Council stands guard at every turn of it: however great the likeness, the unlikeness is greater.

His last word from the cross, in St John, is It is finished. The Latin Bible has Consummatum est. In English a marriage is said to be consummated. That is a chime in the Latin, not an argument from the Greek, and I lean no weight on it. I only say that I can no longer hear the one word without the other.

The French have a name for the summit of love: la petite mort, the little death. I lean on it no harder than on the Latin. But lovers have always known what it names: that to give the body whole is a kind of dying, a going out of oneself with nothing kept back, and that a life may come of it. The marriage bed is a little death, and a sowing. The cross is the great death, and the great sowing. St Paul says it of every grave: It is sown in corruption; it is raised in incorruption.

Then follow the myrrh. In the Song it is the smell of the wedding. The bride rises to open with her fingers dripping myrrh on the handles of the lock, and the garden her beloved comes down into is planted with myrrh and aloes, with all the chief spices. The Psalm of the royal wedding says of the king, All thy garments smell of myrrh, and aloes, and cassia. Now go to Golgotha. They offer him wine mingled with myrrh, and he will not take it. When he is dead Nicodemus comes with a mixture of myrrh and aloes, about an hundred pound weight, and they wind the body in linen with the spices; and St John adds, as if it were nothing, Now in the place where he was crucified there was a garden. Aloes are named only once in the New Testament, and it is here, beside the myrrh. The Bridegroom is dressed for his wedding at last, in the garments of the Psalm, and laid in a garden, behind a door.

And it is in that garden, on the first morning, when it was yet dark, that a woman comes looking for him, as the bride does in the Song: I sought him, but I found him not. She takes him for the gardener, until she hears the bridegroom’s voice, and it is saying her name. I leaned on that moment in the first reflection. I had not noticed what he says to her next. Touch me not; for I am not yet ascended to my Father. Not yet. It is the word of Cana again, and of my title, spoken by the Bridegroom to a woman in a garden, on the morning the door was opened.

Neither marry, nor are given in marriage

The Sadducees once brought Our Lord a riddle about rival husbands. It is a grim little story. A woman is married to seven brothers in turn, as the Law required when a man died childless; she buries all seven, and last of all the woman died also. Therefore in the resurrection whose wife shall she be of the seven? for they all had her. It is the rival wives’ question turned round: there is one of her and seven claims, and someone will have to go without. His answer is not to pick one. In the resurrection they neither marry, nor are given in marriage, but are as the angels of God in heaven.

I do not hear that as a demotion of marriage. I hear it as the end of scarcity. On earth a marriage must be to one, and must keep a door, because we are finite: I cannot give the whole of myself to two people, and the one I try it with will be a tsarah to the other. The exclusiveness of marriage is not a fence around something small. It is the shape a total gift has to take in a creature. That is why the Law forbade sister wives, and why Our Lord, asked about divorce, went back past Moses to the beginning, and they twain shall be one flesh. One, because a creature has only one whole self to give. A husband and wife who keep their door are not hoarding. They are telling the truth about what they are.

But there is one Bridegroom of whom it is not true. Here I want to borrow from something I have worked on for a long time, on being as a gift. Everything that exists has received its being; nothing gave it to itself. To receive is the bride’s part, and so, before God, every creature stands as the bride stands before the bridegroom: men and women, married and single, the old and the unborn. And to receive is not the lower place. When I wrote on the sheep and the goats I said it of the child, who can only receive, and who is given the best room in the house: he stands where the Son stands, who receives everything he has from the Father, for as the Father hath life in himself; so hath he given to the Son to have life in himself. The bride stands there too. That is why the virgins of the parable can be men as well as women, and why the Apocalypse can call the redeemed, all together, one bride.

And a Bridegroom who is God is not finite. He can do what Jacob could not. He can give the whole of himself to each, and lose nothing by giving it to another, and no one he loves need ever be a rival to anyone else he loves. Leah need not count her sons. St Augustine, remembering how his mother wept for him, put it in one line I have never got over: You care for each one of us as if you cared for him alone, and for all of us as for each one. The argument that made rivals of Leah and Rachel has no hold here. It rested on one premise: that the giver is finite, so that a whole gift leaves nothing over. Take the premise away and the conclusion goes with it. One Bridegroom, given wholly to many souls, makes no rivals. The same arrangement that is a tragedy in Jacob’s tent is, in God, the kingdom.

Even mathematics, which only counts, loses its footing here. In 1638 Galileo, an old man under house arrest, set down a thing that troubled him. There are as many square numbers as there are numbers: every number has its square, one for one, though most numbers are not squares, and the squares thin out the further you count. A part had come out as large as its whole. He concluded that greater, less and equal are words for finite things, and fall silent before the infinite. Two and a half centuries later Dedekind made the scandal a definition: a collection is infinite when a part of it can be matched, one for one, with the whole of it.

Hilbert told it to his students as a story. There is a hotel with infinitely many rooms, and every one is taken. A traveller comes at midnight, and the door is not shut. Each guest moves up one room, and the first room stands free. Then a coach pulls in with travellers without number. Each guest moves to the room numbered twice his own, and every odd room down the endless corridor stands empty. The house is full, and there is room. No one at that desk has ever had to say, go ye rather to them that sell.

I do not say that God is a very large number; that would set him at the top of the series again. Cantor, who built the mathematics of the infinite, would not say it either. He proved that even the counting infinity has walls: a coach carrying as many travellers as there are points on a line would not fit in Hilbert’s hotel. Beyond all his infinities he left an Absolute that no number reaches, and gave it to God, and wrote to a cardinal in Rome to show that his numbers did not trespass on it. But if even the infinity of counting can take in a coachload without number and put no one out, then the arithmetic of Jacob’s tent was never going to hold at the door of the kingdom. The Church sings it over the Bread at Corpus Christi: sumit unus, sumunt mille. One receives, a thousand receive, and the one receives as much as the thousand.

The Song of Songs knew it. In its sixth chapter the king surveys his house: There are threescore queens, and fourscore concubines, and virgins without number. My dove, my undefiled is but one. And then the line I would put over the door of every church: The daughters saw her, and blessed her; yea, the queens and the concubines, and they praised her. A house of women in which the others bless the beloved instead of vexing her. No such house has ever stood on earth. It is a picture of the Church, the one dove made of virgins without number, in which each is the only one.

Each as the only one

And what of us, towards one another, in that kingdom where no one is given in marriage? I think this is where Our Lord’s answer to the Sadducees is most generous, and where we hear it least. If there is no marrying there, it is not because we shall love less. It is because we shall at last be able to give ourselves to everyone, and shut no one out, without anyone being robbed.

Dante saw this more clearly than any theologian I know. On the way up from the terrace of Purgatory where the envious sit with their eyes sewn shut, he asks Virgil the question every rival wife has asked: how can a good that is shared out among many make each of them richer than if a few had it? Virgil answers that the goods we fight over are lessened by company, and that is why envy works the bellows of our sighs. But the good that is above is not like that:

For there, as much the more as one says ‘Our,’
So much the more of good each one possesses,
And more of charity in that cloister burns.

In that cloister. Dante’s word for heaven is the word for a convent’s enclosure. And Virgil tells him why it is so. The good of heaven, he says, runs to love as light runs to a bright thing; the more souls there are who love, the more there is to love, and each gives the light back to the others as a mirror does. That is the answer to the oil. The wise virgins were right about oil: a flask is a flask, and what is in yours is not in mine. But what the lamps were for was light, and light is not divided by being shared. I said of the King who hands over his kingdom that he loses nothing by it, as one flame lights another and burns no less. It is the same fire.

And when Dante rises into the heavens and the blessed come streaming towards him, a thousand lights at once, they do not see a stranger who will take a share of what they have. From every one of them he hears the same words: Ecco chi crescerà li nostri amori, here is one who will make our loves greater. Every newcomer at the door is an increase. No one in Dante’s heaven says lest there be not enough for us and you.

There is one man in the Gospels who had every reason to be a rival, and was not. The crowds were leaving John the Baptist for Jesus, and John’s disciples came to him to complain of it: all men come to him. John answered with a wedding. He that hath the bride is the bridegroom: but the friend of the bridegroom, which standeth and heareth him, rejoiceth greatly because of the bridegroom’s voice: this my joy therefore is fulfilled. He must increase, but I must decrease. A man who is made less, and whose joy is full. That is the rival wife’s sorrow undone, in one man, on this side of the grave.

Notice where John stands, for it is how Dante’s blessed love one another. He does not stand between the bridegroom and the bride. He stands beside, and listens, and is glad. The blessed do not turn to face each other, as a husband and wife do, each the other’s whole world. They stand side by side, all turned towards one light, and they see each other in it. I want to be careful here, because it is easy to say something lovely and false. The whole and face-to-face gift of a person, the gift that on earth makes rivals, is given there to God alone; and it can be, because he can receive us all without dividing. Suppose it were otherwise, and each soul in heaven were given wholly to every other, as a spouse is given. We shall still be creatures there, and finite, and the old arithmetic would come back with us: rivals everywhere, as in Jacob’s tent. But set the same souls side by side, each given to every other in the one they all see, and there is nothing to divide. What I give you there I take from no one, because I give it in him, and he is not used up. That is the communion of saints: not a marriage of everyone to everyone, but everyone given to everyone in him, with no one left outside.

The first soul Dante speaks with in Paradise is Piccarda, a nun who was taken by force out of her convent and married against her will. She is in the lowest heaven, and he asks her whether she does not long for a higher place. She smiles, and says that their will is quieted by charity, so that they want only what they have; and in his will is our peace. A virgin who was given in marriage by violence, and who in the kingdom envies no one. That is the end of the tsarah.

Watch therefore

So I come back to the five outside, with their lamps lit from oil bought at midnight, and the voice through the door. In the first reflection I said that if the parable asked me for an ending, I would have them say their names. I can say now that the thought was not mine. In Esther’s palace a woman came in to the king again only if she were called by name. In the Song the one at the door is called my sister, my love, my dove, my undefiled. Every soul that waits for Christ is betrothed and not yet taken in; that is not the condemnation of the five at the door, it is the condition of all ten. What divides them, as I now hear it, is not that some are known and some are not. It is that five were afraid there would not be enough of him.

Now let the three parables sound together, as they did when he told them. In the first, five virgins are left outside a door. In the second, a servant is cast into the darkness outside. In the third the King speaks from his throne, and what he says is that he was out there too: I was a stranger, and ye took me not in. Two days later he is led out of the city and crucified outside the gate, and darkness falls at noon. That evening a rich man lays him in a tomb, and he rolled a great stone to the door. In our Bibles that is the next door in Matthew after the door of the wedding. It is shut, and the Bridegroom is behind it.

I do not know what became of the five. The parable does not say, and I stop where it stops. But I know where the Bridegroom went when he had finished speaking. He did not stay in the lit house. He went the way the foolish and the unprofitable had gone: out, into the dark, with a door shut on him. And I know what became of that door. On the third day the angel of the Lord descended from heaven, and came and rolled back the stone from the door, and sat upon it. In the Authorized Version that is the last door in Matthew, and the last we see of it, an angel is sitting on the stone.

So the three parables do one sum, and the Passion answers it. The wise count oil. The lord counts silver. The goats count the cost. And the man telling the stories, with two days left, is about to give away the whole of himself, to each, and be no less for it. That was the note above the other three, and I could not hear it until I had all of them under it. The Apocalypse lets us hear where he has stood ever since: Behold, I stand at the door, and knock. In the parable the virgins knock and the bridegroom is within. In the Song and in the Apocalypse it is the other way about.

The parable keeps its warning, and so do I. Matthew’s moral stands: Watch therefore, for ye know neither the day nor the hour wherein the Son of man cometh. There is a door, and it shuts, and a door shut at midnight on a wedding is no small thing. Nothing I have said opens it from our side. I have only noticed what kind of bridegroom stands behind it. He is not Jacob, who had to choose between two sisters and made them enemies. He is the one who can give the whole of himself to each, and has.

The wise were right about their oil. But the Bible knows of oil that did not run out. In a famine a widow at Zarephath had an handful of meal in a barrel, and a little oil in a cruse, and she was gathering two sticks to cook it for herself and her son, and die. A stranger at the gate asked her to feed him first. She did; and the barrel of meal wasted not, neither did the cruse of oil fail. And the valiant wife of the first reading on the Sunday of the talents is one of whom Proverbs says that her candle goeth not out by night, and, two verses on, that she stretcheth out her hand to the poor. I do not think those two verses are strangers to each other.

The last page of the Bible is a wedding too. It ends with the bride at the door, and what she says is not Not so. And the Spirit and the bride say, Come. And let him that heareth say, Come. And let him that is athirst come. And whosoever will, let him take the water of life freely. The water at the end of the book is free, and it does not run out, and the bride’s whole speech is an invitation. It is what the ten were betrothed to become.

Advent is close, and with it the season of waiting at night. Bach wrote a cantata on this parable, Wachet auf, for the last Sunday of a long Lutheran year, and he set it as a love story. In the middle of it the Soul and Jesus sing to each other. Ich warte mit brennendem Öle, she sings: I wait with burning oil. Eröffne den Saal, open the hall; and in the same breath he answers, Ich öffne den Saal, I open it. Then he calls her in, his chosen bride, and they sing the Song of Songs together: Mein Freund ist mein, und ich bin sein, my beloved is mine, and I am his. Bach had no doubt which room the parable ends in, or whose hand is on the door.

Until then it may be worth practising the one sentence the parable gives us to say at a door, Lord, Lord, open to us, and noticing that it is plural. Nobody in the kingdom knocks for themselves alone.
