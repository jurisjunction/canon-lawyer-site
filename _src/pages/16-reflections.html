Musings on Scripture

Occasional reflections on a passage of the Bible. They are a layman’s musings, food for thought and nothing more: not teaching, not the mind of the Church, not a homily, and not a substitute for your parish priest. Like the essays, each is written in the register of a writer I admire, and says so.

  • A layman’s reading, in the prophets’ register · November 2026

    The Beginning of Sorrows

    The end of Mark 13, for the First Sunday of Advent, Year B. A porter told to stay awake; the lights of heaven, the first clocks, put out; birth pangs, not sorrows; the four watches kept as the Passion night, and a man shown; Ignatius on the road to the beasts, who would become a human being; Irenaeus and the image shown; a Red Wednesday poster turned over; and the chants of Ad te levavi.

  • A layman’s reading · November 2026

    I Know You Not (Yet)

    The ten virgins a second time, read from the bridal chamber as a descant over the three Sundays of Matthew 25. Virgins, not bridesmaids, and a wedding with no bride; the rival wives of the Old Testament; what it means to be known; Cana’s “not yet”; the cross as the bridal chamber; and a kingdom where, because no one is given in marriage, no one is a rival.

  • A layman’s reading · November 2026

    The Throne of His Glory

    The sheep and the goats, for the feast of Christ the King. Who is speaking when the King says “me”; a throne that is a cross, with a thief on either side; what glory weighs; a King who reigns by dying and meets each of us in our death; the parents’ school of the six works; and the respectable people on his left. Christus regnat, yes: a ligno.

  • In the register of Dorothy Day · November 2026

    The Unprofitable Servant

    The talents, read for the thirty-third Sunday and the World Day of the Poor, from the back of the house. What doubling silver cost in that world; the one clean way out the Law of Moses left a servant, and Dorothy Day sending back the city’s interest; the kingdom hid in the earth; two masters; and a darkness outside that leads, if you keep reading, to the King among the least.

  • A layman’s reading · November 2026

    I Know You Not

    The ten virgins, read for the thirty-second Sunday. The bridegroom speaks to only five of them, and they are the ones outside the door; the gnashing of teeth is not in the text. Oil as what it is, the stored sunlight of a life that is over. Prudence and abandonment as two wisdoms. And an ending the parable withholds, supplied from the Christ who calls his own by name.

  • In the register of St John Henry Newman · November 2026

    Wholly as Fire

    The road to Emmaus and the great multitude, read together for All Saints. Two men whose hearts were burning before they knew it, a stranger who let them do most of the talking, a supper of broiled fish, and the saints not as suns or moons but as flames, each lit from another: same fire, double light.

  • In the register of G. K. Chesterton · October 2026

    Here, You Have What Is Yours

    The coin of tribute, the buried talent and Peter’s “God forbid” read together, as three ways of handing God back what is his untouched. A graven image produced in the Temple; a servant who lost nothing and was cast out; a God who scatters; and the woman at Bethany, whose three hundred denarii the disciples called waste and Our Lord called beautiful.

  • A parable for the Advent age · October 2026

    O Radix

    Speculative fiction. A judge in a cold city reads the first chapter of John as a graph of origins, asks the small program he built whether a machine can have the mind of Christ, gives the program away on a network with no centre, and types in the sentence that the Logos has pitched his tent in it. The program refuses, twice, and then tells him where the Logos already is.

  • In the register of C. S. Lewis · October 2026

    The Four Soils Are All Yours

    The parable of the sower, read not as a sorting of people into four kinds but as a map of one life, in which the seed lands on every patch at once. Why a life is a web of arrows and not a line, why the harvest from a single good plot is out of all proportion to its size, and when the harvest actually comes.
