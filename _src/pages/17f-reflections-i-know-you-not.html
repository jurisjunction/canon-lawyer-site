Biblical reflections

Reflection · a layman’s reading

I Know You Not

The ten virgins, for the thirty-second Sunday of the year. Who the bridegroom speaks to, what oil is, two kinds of wisdom, and an ending the parable withholds. Food for thought, not teaching; the liberties are marked where they are taken.

Richard Verver · November 2026

The parable of the ten virgins was read at Mass this morning, and the deacon preached it the way it is usually preached: be ready, keep oil in your lamp, for the hour is coming when it will be too late. He is not wrong. That is the moral Matthew himself attaches, Watch therefore, for ye know neither the day nor the hour, and a preacher who gives the evangelist’s own moral has done his duty. But the deacon added a line that the text does not have, and the line has been bothering me all day. He said: they sought, and did not find; they knocked, and it was not opened to them. I want to look at what the parable says, what it does not say, and what, if we must supply an ending, the rest of the Gospel would supply.

Who is spoken to

Read it again and listen only for the bridegroom’s voice. Ten virgins go out to meet him with lamps. Five are prudent and carry oil in flasks besides; five are foolish and carry none. He tarries; all ten nod and sleep. At midnight the cry goes up, and all ten rise and trim their lamps. The foolish ask for oil and are refused; they go to buy; while they are gone he comes, and they that were ready went in with him to the marriage: and the door was shut. The others return and call through the door, Lord, Lord, open to us. And he answers, Verily I say unto you, I know you not.

That is the only thing he says in the whole parable. He never speaks to the wise. They are not greeted, not thanked, not praised for their flasks. They go in, and the door closes behind them, and the text loses interest in them entirely. The one sentence the bridegroom utters, he utters to the five on the wrong side of the door. If you had only his words to go by, you would say the parable was about them.

I do not think this is nothing. In Matthew the same sentence is said twice more, and both times to people who called him Lord. At the end of the Sermon on the Mount, to those who prophesied and cast out devils in his name: I never knew you. And in the courtyard of the high priest, in the mouth of Peter: I know not the man, said twice, the second time with an oath. The formula of the shut door is the formula of Peter’s denial. The bridegroom says to the foolish virgins what Peter said about him. That is a strange thing to put in his mouth, and Matthew put it there.

What is not in the text

Here is what the parable does not say. It does not say the foolish were cast into outer darkness. Matthew has a phrase for that and he uses it six times, weeping and gnashing of teeth; he uses it of the man without a wedding garment two chapters earlier and of the slothful servant in the very next parable. He withholds it here. There is a shut door and a voice. Nothing else. The parable stops with the foolish outside and the bridegroom speaking to them, and it does not tell us what happened next.

The deacon’s line, they knocked and it was not opened, is a conflation of two texts. Knock, and it shall be opened unto you is Matthew 7:7. The shut door is Matthew 25:10. He laid the first over the second and reversed it: here, he said, is the case where the knocking fails. But Matthew did not write that. He wrote knock, and it shall be opened in one place, and a shut door in another, and left the reader to carry the one to the other. The usual sermon carries nothing across; it reads the shut door as final and fills the silence with our own smallness, because a door that stays shut on the undeserving is the kind of door we understand.

I should say plainly that I am not denying judgment. There is a door, and it shuts, and the parable is a warning and was meant as one; the Church reads it in November for a reason. What I am denying is that the text tells us the end of the story of the five outside. It gives us a sentence and then it stops. The parable is aporetic. It leaves us at the door with them.

What oil is

Before going further I want to look at the oil, because the whole parable turns on it and we usually hurry past it to its meaning. Lamp oil in Palestine was olive oil, and olive oil is fat: three long chains of carbon, each tied to one small molecule of glycerol, like three ribbons knotted to one hand. I described the same molecule in the essay on soap, because soap is made of it too. Boil the fat with lye and the knots break: the chains become soap, and the hand, glycerol, goes free into the water. That is one thing you can do with a fat. The other is to burn it.

When a wick draws oil up into a flame, the heat cracks the chains, and the fragments of carbon, glowing in the air before they are consumed, give the yellow light. The light of a lamp is the burning of what the olive made. And what the olive made, it made out of sunlight, in a summer that is over, by the slow chemistry of its leaves, and stored in the fruit against a future it would not see. Every lamp is a little resurrection. It gives back, in the dark, light that fell on a tree a year ago, or ten years ago, or, if the oil has been kept, longer. Oil is the stored sunlight of a life that has ended.

So the two things you can do with a fat answer to two ends, as the soap essay said of its bar: break it, and it cleans; keep it whole and burn it, and it gives light. The parable is at a wedding, in the dark, and the end is light. Zechariah saw it: a lampstand of gold, and two olive trees beside it, and the oil running from the trees to the lamps through pipes, and when he asked what the trees were he was told, These are the two anointed ones, that stand by the Lord of the whole earth. The oil comes from the anointed. Christos means anointed. The lamp burns him.

And a lamp holds very little. The bowl of a clay lamp burns for an hour or two; the flask the wise carried was the reserve against delay. The foolish did not lack lamps, and they did not lack faith; they went out to meet him. What they lacked was the stored past. They brought themselves and nothing else.

Two wisdoms

Matthew calls the five with flasks phronimoi, and I do not want to take the word from them. It is the word for the man who built his house on rock, and for the faithful steward, and the evangelist means it as praise. But it is a particular kind of wisdom. Phronēsis is prudence: the practical reason that foresees, provides, and keeps something back. It is the wisdom Our Lord praises in the unjust steward when he says the children of this world are in their generation wiser than the children of light, and the same word is in that sentence. When the foolish ask for oil and the wise say Not so; lest there be not enough for us and you: but go ye rather to them that sell, and buy for yourselves, that is not malice. It is prudence talking. It is also exactly what prudence always says.

The other five are called mōrai. It is the word Paul uses of himself: we are fools for Christ’s sake, mōroi. I am not going to pretend the parable intends the compliment; Matthew’s other fool is the man who built on sand. But I notice what their folly consisted in. They did not plan for his delay. They went out to meet a bridegroom with a lamp and no reserve, as if he would come when he said and the lamp would do. Their folly was to take the promise of his coming at face value and bring nothing of their own to cover the gap. Call it what it was: an abandonment to his arrival. It is imprudent. It is also the posture of every saint who ever set out with nothing, and the one Our Lord asked of the Twelve when he sent them without purse or scrip.

So there are two wisdoms in the parable, and the text rewards one of them with the feast and gives the other a word. The prudent bring the past; they carry, in a flask, light that fell on an olive tree in another year, and they are right to, and they go in. The fools bring only the present, which is to say only themselves, and are left at the door. And then he speaks to them.

The ending the parable does not give

Now the liberty, marked as one. I know you not is a refusal; in the Greek and in every language it means what it says. But it is also, in every language, how an acquaintance begins. I do not know you. Then tell me who you are. The parable breaks off at exactly the point where a conversation would start, and I think the break is the point. Matthew leaves the door shut and the sentence hanging and does not say what the five answered.

If we must supply what they answered, we should supply it from the portrait of Christ the Gospels give, not from our own experience of doors. And that portrait is unambiguous about names. I am the good shepherd, and know my sheep, and am known of mine. He calleth his own sheep by name, and leadeth them out. In the garden, a woman weeping at a shut tomb takes him for the gardener until he says one word, Mary, and her eyes open. Zacchaeus is called by name out of a tree. Simon is given a new one. Being known by name is, in this Gospel and the others, the whole of what it means to be his.

So the secret ending, the one I would write if the parable asked me to, is that the five said their names. I know you not. Lord, I am so-and-so, the daughter of so-and-so, from such a village, and I came out to meet you with a lamp and no oil, because I thought you would come when you said. And he knew them by name. That is not in the text. It fits the Christ of the text better than the gnashing of teeth that is not in the text either. Of the two eisegeses, I will take the one that looks like him.

Read this way, the two halves of the parable do not contradict. The wise go in on the strength of what they carried, and they are right, and the feast is real. The foolish go in, if they go in, on the strength of being known, with nothing in their hands, which is the only way anyone finally goes in. The flask is a good thing to have. It is not the thing that is loved.

Watch

Matthew’s moral stands: watch. But notice that nobody in the parable watched. All ten slept. The wise were not more wakeful; they were better provisioned. The parable is not about the length of the vigil but about the moment of waking, and what you have on you when the cry goes up. And on that there are two answers in the text, not one. Have oil: the stored light of a life handed to you, the past kept against the delay, the inheritance. And, if at midnight you find you have none, go to the door anyway, and when he says he does not know you, tell him your name.

I am a canonist and a layman and no preacher, and this is offered as food for thought, the way the reflections on this page always are. The deacon gave the moral Matthew gave. I only wanted to say that the text ends sooner than his sermon did, and that in the space it leaves, the Christ of the rest of the Gospel is the better guide to what happened next.
