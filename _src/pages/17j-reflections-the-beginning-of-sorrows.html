Biblical reflections

Reflection · a layman’s reading, in the prophets’ register

The Beginning of Sorrows

The end of Mark 13, for the First Sunday of Advent, Year B (29 November 2026), read with the chants of the day, with St Ignatius of Antioch and St Irenaeus of Lyon, with Fr John Behr, and with a poster for a Mass. Who is awake, what no clock can tell, and what is shown when the covering comes off. Written in something of the prophets’ register, after Abraham Joshua Heschel. Food for thought, not teaching; the liberties are marked where they are taken.

Richard Verver · November 2026

The porter

On Wednesday the eighteenth of November, eleven days before this Sunday, St Michael’s Cathedral in Toronto kept Red Wednesday with a Mass for persecuted Christians. I am glad it did. The poster for it showed a man. He is not young. He wears a blue checked shirt, there is sweat on his face, and he looks straight out of the picture. Behind him is a wall of rough boards lit red, with a red cloth hung across one corner and a crucifix above it. I do not know his name or his country; the poster does not give them. Across the red, in white letters, it asks: Who stands with persecuted Christians?

It is a good question, and before the end I mean to answer it. But there is something odd in it, and the Gospel of this Sunday turns it over. The question has us for its subject. We are the ones who might stand, and he is the one to be stood with. We have the cathedral and the evening free, and he has need of us. Mark 13 was spoken to men like him, and it does not see the matter that way round.

The prophets, as Abraham Joshua Heschel taught me to read them, were men who could not sleep. What the rest of us get used to kept them awake. Israel had a name for that office. Son of man, I have made thee a watchman unto the house of Israel. One of them heard a voice call out of Seir, Watchman, what of the night? and he answered, The morning cometh, and also the night. That is the office our Lord hands on in the last word of this chapter, and he does not hand it to prophets only: what I say unto you I say unto all, Watch. Advent begins by making a watchman of every Christian. So it is fair to ask, in a city that gives the persecuted one evening a year, who is in fact awake.

A week ago the Church closed her year with a King on a throne that turned out to be a cross. This Sunday she opens the new one, and the first Gospel of the year is the end of the world.

It begins with a man admiring a building. Master, see what manner of stones and what buildings are here! Our Lord answers that there shall not be left one stone upon another, that shall not be thrown down, and then he sits down on the Mount of Olives, across the valley from the Temple, and gives his last long teaching before he dies.

Notice where we are, because we have been here before. The last three Sundays of the old year read us the twenty-fifth chapter of Matthew: the ten virgins, the talents, the sheep and the goats. That chapter too is spoken as he sat upon the mount of Olives, and when it is over Matthew says, Ye know that after two days is the feast of the passover, and the Son of man is betrayed to be crucified. Mark’s chapter is followed by After two days was the feast of the passover. It is one teaching, on one hill, two days before the Passion, told by two evangelists. The Church closes one year and opens the next without leaving the Mount of Olives. This Sunday she reads us the last five verses of Mark’s telling.

For the Son of man is as a man taking a far journey, who left his house, and gave authority to his servants, and to every man his work, and commanded the porter to watch. Watch ye therefore: for ye know not when the master of the house cometh, at even, or at midnight, or at the cockcrowing, or in the morning: Lest coming suddenly he find you sleeping. And what I say unto you I say unto all, Watch.

That is the whole of it: a house, a master gone away, a porter at the door, and a night cut into four watches, as the Romans cut it. Each servant has his work. Only the porter is told what his work is, and it is to stay awake.

We have met this master. Two Sundays ago Matthew’s parable of the talents began, For the kingdom of heaven is as a man travelling into a far country, who called his own servants, and delivered unto them his goods. In the Greek, Matthew’s man and Mark’s are described in the same words: an anthrōpos going abroad. The Sunday before that, the ten virgins ended on the porter’s own word: Watch therefore, for ye know neither the day nor the hour wherein the Son of man cometh. The year has turned, and the same man is still away, and his servants are still told to stay awake.

I want to ask a question of this passage that it does not ask of itself. Chapters like this one are called apocalypses, from the Greek apokalypsis, an uncovering. What would it be for the whole of it to be uncovered: not one secret, but everything at once, each thing shown with what it stood on?

Programmers have a word for the pile of everything a running program stands on at a given moment: the stack. When a program halts, it can print the whole pile, each call with the call beneath it, down to where it began. That is an image, so to speak, and I mark it as one; nothing in Mark is software. But it is close to what the old word means. On the last day nothing is hidden, and nothing is shown alone.

Mark’s answer to the question is stranger than a list of everything. When the covering comes off, what is shown is a man. The rest of this reflection tries to follow that: from the lights of heaven going out, through a birth, to a man whom a Roman governor presents to a crowd, and to an old bishop on his way to the beasts, who wrote that when he got there he would become a human being. Then it comes back to the man on the poster.

The lights go out

The Sunday’s portion is short, but it stands at the end of a passage the Church has read us on other Sundays, and it cannot be read without it. A few verses earlier comes the heart of the chapter. But in those days, after that tribulation, the sun shall be darkened, and the moon shall not give her light, And the stars of heaven shall fall, and the powers that are in heaven shall be shaken.

We hear that as the furniture of disaster, like an eclipse in a film. Hear it instead against the first page of the Bible. On the fourth day God made the lights of heaven, and he said what they were for: for signs, and for seasons, and for days, and years. The greater light to rule the day, and the lesser light to rule the night: he made the stars also. The sun, the moon and the stars are the first clocks. They are how there come to be days, months and years at all. Mark names the same three, in the same order, and puts them out.

What is left when the clocks go out? Not nothing. The clocks were laid on something: an order of things that come before and after, a beginning and an end. The calendar is a measure laid on that order, the way a ruler is laid on a line; take the ruler away and the line is still there. A few verses on, our Lord says so himself. Heaven and earth shall pass away: but my words shall not pass away. The whole created order, the house and everything in it, is one thing, with a beginning, and in the form it has now it will pass away; the word that holds it does not.

And then he says the one thing a clock cannot tell. But of that day and that hour knoweth no man, no, not the angels which are in heaven, neither the Son, but the Father. Whatever we make of the last words, and the Church has said much about them, the first ones are plain. The end is not on the calendar. It is not only that the date is kept from us. The day on which the measures fail cannot be measured by them. Every attempt to fix it, and there have been many, sincere and learned and wrong, mistakes the clock for the order it measures.

We go on trying. In September of last year a preacher’s date for the Rapture went round the world on TikTok, and some who believed him said they had quit their jobs and sold what they owned. The day came and went. Amos had a word for people in a hurry: Woe unto you that desire the day of the LORD! to what end is it for you? the day of the LORD is darkness, and not light.

The learned keep a clock too. Since 1947 the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists has set a Doomsday Clock, and this January it put the hands at eighty-five seconds to midnight, the nearest they have ever stood. I do not mock it. Its keepers call it a metaphor, and the dangers it counts are real. But notice what kind of clock it is. Its hands have been moved back as well as forward. It tells how near we have brought ourselves to a midnight of our own making, and men can estimate that, because it is a thing men do. That is a warning, and a warning can be heeded. The day our Lord speaks of is not ours to hasten or to put off. It can only be watched for.

So the instruction is not to calculate. It is Watch. A man who cannot know the hour can still be awake at any hour. That is the porter’s whole office, and it is a strange one: he is not asked to do anything to bring the master back, nor to guess when he will come, but only to be the kind of man who is at the door when he does.

The beginning of sorrows

Earlier still, the chapter lists the things that frighten us: wars, earthquakes, famines. Be ye not troubled, our Lord says; for such things must needs be; but the end shall not be yet. And then a phrase that the Authorized Version makes sound like a sigh. These are the beginnings of sorrows.

The Greek is plainer and more particular. The word is ōdinōn, the pains of a woman in labour; Mark says archē ōdinōn, the beginning of birth pangs. The old translators were not wrong to write sorrows, for labour is sorrow. But they lost the one thing the word knows that the English does not: what the pain is for. The end of the world, as Mark tells it, is not a demolition. It is a delivery.

St Paul uses the same word, as a verb, for the whole of creation. For we know that the whole creation groaneth and travaileth in pain together until now. And he says what it is waiting for. For the earnest expectation of the creature waiteth for the manifestation of the sons of God. The English has manifestation; the Greek has apokalypsis again. The thing the world is in labour to uncover is not a secret or a date. It is sons.

The second reading this Sunday says the same of the Son. St Paul thanks God for the Corinthians, who come behind in no gift; waiting for the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ. Here too the Greek for coming is apokalypsis. The same word twice: what is waited for is the uncovering of a person.

What does a labour end in? St John answers in the words of our Lord on the night before he died. A woman when she is in travail hath sorrow, because her hour is come: but as soon as she is delivered of the child, she remembereth no more the anguish, for joy that a man is born into the world. The word for a man is anthrōpos: not a male, but a human being. And her hour is come is the phrase our Lord has been using of himself all through that Gospel: The hour is come, that the Son of man should be glorified.

Notice what the image does not do. A woman in labour knows the birth is near, but the pains do not keep a timetable. They are a beginning, and they say only that the end is coming, not when. That is exactly the use our Lord makes of the fig tree a few verses on: when ye shall see these things come to pass, know that it is nigh, even at the doors. Signs give an order: this, then that. They do not give a date.

And notice what he says about the news. When ye shall hear of wars and rumours of wars, be ye not troubled. No generation has heard of more wars than ours, or sooner. They reach us on a telephone before breakfast, and the rumours come with them. He does not say, be ye not moved. The trouble he forbids is the kind that ends in sleep. St Luke says that in the garden our Lord found the disciples sleeping for sorrow. There is a wakefulness that is only that sleep under another name. A man can lie awake until two with the grief of the whole world lit in his hand, and be no more a watchman than the man who snores. The woman in labour is not watching her pain. She is waiting for a child.

So here is the first answer to the question I asked. When the whole of it is uncovered, what is uncovered is a birth, and what is born is a human being. The rest of Mark tells us who.

Behold the man

Go back to the porter’s four watches, and then turn the page. Mark has just told the parable of a night; within two chapters he tells the night itself, and it keeps the same hours. And in the evening he cometh with the twelve: that is the supper. Later, in the garden, he cometh, and findeth them sleeping, the very words of the warning: lest coming suddenly he find you sleeping. Then the second time the cock crew, and Peter wept. Then straightway in the morning they bound him and delivered him to Pilate. At even, at midnight, at the cockcrowing, in the morning. The master of the house kept every watch of that night, and his servants failed him in every one: at supper one of them had already sold him, in the garden they slept, at cockcrow Peter denied him, and before morning all of them had fled.

The Greek has one more thing to show. In the parable, the master is as a man taking a far journey, and the word is anthrōpos again. Hold on to it.

The next day there was darkness over the whole land, from the sixth hour to the ninth. The sun, the first of the clocks, goes out, and at midday, as Amos had said it would: I will cause the sun to go down at noon, and I will darken the earth in the clear day. Then our Lord cries with a loud voice and gives up the ghost, And the veil of the temple was rent in twain from the top to the bottom. The verb is the one Mark used at the beginning of his Gospel, when Jesus came up out of the Jordan and saw the heavens opened: in Greek, torn. The first reading this Sunday is Isaiah’s cry, Oh that thou wouldest rend the heavens, that thou wouldest come down. Mark answers it twice, at the river and at the cross.

The veil was not plain cloth. Josephus, who knew the Temple before it fell, describes a curtain at its entrance, a Babylonian tapestry in blue, scarlet and purple, worked with a picture of the heavens. Whether that is the curtain Mark means, scholars dispute. If it is, then the sun, the moon and the stars that Mark puts out in chapter 13 were woven into the cloth that tears in chapter 15. Josephus says it showed everything in the heavens except the signs of the zodiac. But the veil that hid the holy of holies was there to cover what no one might see, and the letter to the Hebrews says what it was: the veil, that is to say, his flesh. When the covering comes off, what is uncovered is a body.

Isaiah’s cry has a sister, a few chapters earlier, which the Church sings all through Advent. Drop down, ye heavens, from above, and let the skies pour down righteousness: let the earth open, and let them bring forth salvation. St Jerome’s Latin, which is what the chant sings, hears persons where the Hebrew has gifts: Rorate caeli desuper, et nubes pluant iustum; aperiatur terra, et germinet Salvatorem. Let the clouds rain down the Just One; let the earth be opened, and bud forth a Saviour. It is the introit of the last Sunday of Advent. When it was later made into the Advent prose, one of its verses was taken from this Sunday’s reading, nearly word for word: we all do fade as a leaf, thou hast hid thy face from us. So one prophet’s two cries frame the season: rend the heavens, at its start, and drop down, ye heavens, at its end. The veil in Mark tears from the top. The clouds of the Rorate are the clouds the Son of man is to come in. And the earth that opens to bud forth a Saviour is the earth that, in this Sunday’s communion, yields her increase. The heavens open from above and the earth from below, and between them is one man.

And someone sees it. And when the centurion, which stood over against him, saw that he so cried out, and gave up the ghost, he said, Truly this man was the Son of God. In the Greek, this man is houtos ho anthrōpos. It is the first time in Mark that a human being calls Jesus the Son of God, and it is a Roman soldier, at a death, not at a miracle. The anthrōpos of the parable is the one who goes away. Here, in the hour of his going, a stranger sees him for what he is.

St John says it outright, and puts it in Pilate’s mouth. Jesus comes out wearing the crown of thorns and the purple robe, and Pilate says, Behold the man! A little later, on the cross, Jesus says, It is finished, and gives up the ghost. The Orthodox patristics scholar Fr John Behr has written much on these two sayings. He reads them against the first page of Genesis. There God makes everything else by a word, but over the human being he deliberates: Let us make man in our image, after our likeness. On the sixth day, the day of man, the heavens and the earth were finished. On another sixth day, a Friday, the governor shows the crowd a man, and the man says, It is finished. What God set out to make at the beginning is shown, complete, on the cross. Christ, Behr says, is the first true human being. Adam was the figure of him that was to come, as St Paul calls him, a first sketch. Christ is not Plan B.

Two cautions, because this is easy to say wrongly. The first: our Lord did not become a man on the cross. The Word was made flesh when he was conceived, and from then on he was as human as any of us, and more. What was finished on Calvary was not his nature but his course: Though he were a Son, yet learned he obedience by the things which he suffered; And being made perfect, he became the author of eternal salvation. The cross does not make a man. It shows one, finished.

The second: the cross is not the last day. The darkness, the torn veil and the soldier’s confession show the end early, in sign, the way a flash shows a landscape at night. That is my reading, and I mark it as one. The master has not yet come back in the clouds. Ye shall see the Son of man sitting on the right hand of power, and coming in the clouds of heaven, Jesus told the high priest, and we have not seen it. So we keep the watch. But we know now whom we are watching for, and what he looks like when the covering comes off.

God’s wheat

Some seventy-five years after the crucifixion, give or take, the bishop of Antioch was arrested and sent to Rome to die in the arena. His name was Ignatius; he called himself Theophorus, the God-bearer. He went overland under guard, chained, he said, to ten leopards, by which he meant the soldiers. On the way he wrote seven letters to the churches. One of them went ahead of him to Rome, and in it he begged the Christians there not to use their influence to save him.

It is a hard letter to read, and it is meant to be. I am God’s wheat, he writes, and I am ground by the teeth of the beasts, that I may be found Christ’s pure bread. And then, a little further on, the passage this reflection turns on. The translation is mine, and close.

The birth is upon me. Bear with me, brethren. Do not keep me from living; do not wish me to die. … Let me receive the pure light. When I have come there, I shall be a human being. Let me be an imitator of the passion of my God.

The Greek of the second sentence is anthrōpos esomai: I shall be a man, a human being. Everything in the passage is turned inside out. To be rescued would be to die; to be killed is to live. To go into the arena is to be born. And what is born, when he gets there, is a human being, as if until then he had not quite been one.

Fr Behr starts his account of what it is to be human from exactly this sentence. Ignatius, he says, speaks as one not yet born, not yet living, not yet human, who will be born into life as a human being only through his martyrdom, in imitation of Christ. Behind it he hears St John’s Gospel, and a Lord who called his own death his hour, and a woman’s hour, and the birth of a man into the world. The end of the world in Mark is a labour whose child is a human being. The end of one man’s world, in Ignatius, is the same.

Notice the light. Mark puts out the sun, the moon and the stars, the lights that measure. Ignatius asks for pure light, a light that measures nothing. St John saw it at the end of his book: a city that had no need of the sun, neither of the moon, to shine in it: for the glory of God did lighten it, and the Lamb is the light thereof. When the clocks go out, it is not dark.

This is not a taste for death. Ignatius does not want to die; he wants to live, and he has come to believe that the life he wants lies on the far side of the beasts, because it lay on the far side of a cross. Let me be an imitator of the passion of my God. Few of us will be asked for it in an arena. Fr Behr finds the same shape in marriage, where two people learn to lay their lives down for each other daily. Our Lord put it without an arena: Whosoever shall seek to save his life shall lose it; and whosoever shall lose his life shall preserve it.

And notice the bread. A grain of wheat goes into the ground, as I wrote in I Know You Not (Yet), and the ground gives back much fruit. Ignatius goes further, into the mill and the oven. He means to be eaten. Hold on to that too; the chants of this Sunday end with it.

One thing needs a guard. Ignatius says I shall be a human being. He does not say that he was not one, or that anyone is not. What that difference is, St Irenaeus says better than anyone.

The work of thy hand

Irenaeus grew up in Smyrna, where as a boy he listened to the old bishop Polycarp, who had known St John; Polycarp, in his turn, had been sent a letter by Ignatius on his way to Rome. Irenaeus went west, to Gaul, and became bishop of Lyon after the martyrs of 177. Around 180 he wrote five books against the Gnostics, who held that the body was a prison and the world a mistake. In 2022 Pope Francis named him a Doctor of the Church, the Doctor of unity: a bishop from the East who taught in the West.

He says in one sentence what the uncovering is. For in times long past, it was said that man was created after the image of God, but it was not [actually] shown; for the Word was as yet invisible, after whose image man was created. … When, however, the Word of God became flesh, He confirmed both these: for He both showed forth the image truly, since He became Himself what was His image; and He re-established the similitude. We were made in the image of the Son. Until the Son had a face, nobody had seen the original. When he took flesh, the picture and the one it pictures stood in the same place, and for the first time you could see what a human being is meant to look like.

Irenaeus has a name for the Son and the Spirit: the Hands of God. It was to them, he says, that the Father spoke when he said Let us make man. And the Hands do not let go of their work. At the end of the story, he writes, they perfected a living human being: the Latin is vivum perfecerunt hominem. Listen to how the first reading this Sunday ends: But now, O LORD, thou art our father; we are the clay, and thou our potter; and we all are the work of thy hand. Isaiah has just complained, thou hast hid thy face from us. He ends by trusting the hand. The clay is still on the wheel.

That is why Irenaeus can say, without embarrassment, that Adam was a child. God could have made man perfect from the first, as a mother could give a newborn solid food; but the newborn cannot take it. We were made to grow. The image was given at the start, and no one can take it away; the likeness is what the image grows into, and it is not finished yet.

Here is the guard I promised, and on this site it has to be said plainly. When Ignatius says I shall be a human being, he is speaking of the end we are made for, not of the nature we have. A child in the womb is a human being. So is a man with dementia, and a man serving life for murder, and a man who has done terrible things, and a woman who has been told she is a burden, or has told herself so. The Church’s most recent teaching on human dignity, Dignitas infinita (2024), puts it in modern terms. It distinguishes the ontological dignity that belongs to every person simply because he exists, which can never be lost, from moral dignity, which is how we use our freedom, and which can be. It calls everyone to show in the way they live the dignity they already have. Irenaeus’s image and likeness say the same thing in older words. Ignatius did not become more of a person in the arena. He became what persons are for.

I write this in a country that needs the guard. In 2024, by Health Canada’s count, 16,499 people here died by what our law calls medical assistance in dying: about one death in twenty. I do not judge one of them. I do not know what any of them carried, and it is not mine to weigh. But a people can be weighed, and we are the people. Dignitas infinita says that suffering does not take away the dignity of the sick, which is their own and cannot be given up or lost. A country that prints that number once a year in a report, and is not kept awake by it, is asleep on its watch.

And what are they for? Irenaeus’s most famous sentence is usually quoted half. Gloria enim Dei vivens homo, vita autem hominis visio Dei: the glory of God is a living human being, and the life of a human being is the vision of God. A human being is fully alive, in the end, by seeing.

In the same passage Irenaeus adds something that keeps the whole picture straight. The Word, he says, reveals God to man and presents man to God, preserving at the same time the invisibility of the Father, lest man should at any time become a despiser of God, and that he should always possess something towards which he might advance. Even when everything is uncovered, the Father is not one more thing uncovered. To keep my own image honest: God is not the bottom layer of the pile. He is not in it at all. Seeing him is not reaching a floor; it is going toward someone, without end. It doth not yet appear what we shall be, St John writes, but we know that, when he shall appear, we shall be like him; for we shall see him as he is.

Irenaeus’s great word for what Christ does is the one St Paul uses when he says that in the fulness of times God will gather together in one all things in Christ. The Greek is anakephalaiōsasthai, to bring all things under one head. That is the last thing to say about the uncovering. When the whole of it is shown, it is not shown as a heap. It is shown with its head.

Who stands

Now the poster again, and its question.

Mark 13 has a word for the persecuted. This Sunday’s portion leaves it out; it stands a few verses back, with the wars. They shall deliver you up to councils; and in the synagogues ye shall be beaten: and ye shall be brought before rulers and kings for my sake, for a testimony against them. The Greek for ye shall be brought is stathēsesthe: you shall be made to stand. The Greek for testimony is martyrion. And they are told not to prepare a speech: whatsoever shall be given you in that hour, that speak ye: for it is not ye that speak, but the Holy Ghost. The hour of the end is kept from everyone. This hour is given, and what to say in it.

So in the chapter itself the persecuted are not a cause. They are the ones standing. They are the Church doing in public what the Church is for, and the name for it is the word we get martyr from. The warnings about sleep are meant for somebody else: for the servants indoors, in the house where nothing seems to be happening.

Ignatius knew which he was. His letter to Rome is a letter from a persecuted Christian to the church in the capital, which had some influence there and wanted very much to stand with him. He asks them to stand down. If you keep silence about me, he writes, I am a word of God; but if you love my flesh, I shall be only a voice again. Then he tells them what he does want. Only ask strength for me, within and without, that I may not only say it but will it; that I may not only be called a Christian, but be found one. He does not ask the Romans to feel for him. He asks them to pray that he be found a Christian, and he leaves them, without saying so, to wonder whether they would be.

That is no licence to leave the persecuted to it. The letter to the Hebrews is plain: Remember them that are in bonds, as bound with them; and them which suffer adversity, as being yourselves also in the body. A week ago the King on his throne said, I was in prison, and ye came unto me, and then, Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me. On the Damascus road he asked Saul, why persecutest thou me? The persecuted are owed bread, and a roof, and someone to speak for them where laws are made, and our prayers. Aid to the Church in Need exists for that. Pray for them by name. Give.

But count them first. Aid to the Church in Need’s report for 2025 finds religious freedom gravely violated in 62 of the world’s 196 countries, home to some 5.4 billion people of every faith, nearly two in every three of us; in 24 of those countries it calls what is happening persecution. A number that size is another clock. It tells us how late it is and lets us go back to sleep. So take one night.

On the night of the thirteenth of June 2025, armed men came into Yelwata, in Benue State in Nigeria, a village that is almost wholly Catholic. Families already driven from their homes were sheltering there with the Catholic mission, and many were sleeping in lodgings on the market square. The men set fire to the doors. About two hundred people died; the counts differ. At the parish church, where some hundreds more were asleep, the police turned the attackers back. Pope Leo spoke of it at the Angelus that Sunday and called it a terrible massacre. The parish priest lived through the night, and he stayed. “God has not abandoned us,” he told Aid to the Church in Need.

That is a porter. The master of the house was away, they came in the night, and the man at the door did not leave it. The warning about sleep was never for the people on that square. They slept as the tired and the hunted sleep, and he that keepeth Israel shall neither slumber nor sleep. The warning is for us, who heard of that night, if we heard of it at all, between one thing and the next.

Now turn the poster’s question over. Who stands with persecuted Christians? In Mark’s Passion the answer is: nobody. Peter had said, If I should die with thee, I will not deny thee in any wise, and Mark adds, Likewise also said they all. When the hour came, they all forsook him, and fled. The women were looking on afar off. The one man of whom Mark says that he stood over against him was the centurion, the officer in charge of the killing. He is the one who stood, and he saw.

And yet the question has an answer, and St Luke gives it. When Stephen, the first of the martyrs, was made to stand before the council, he looked up stedfastly into heaven, and saw the glory of God, and Jesus standing on the right hand of God. Wherever else the Scriptures give his posture, he is seated: so he told the high priest, and so we say in the Creed. For Stephen he is on his feet. St Gregory the Great, preaching on the Ascension, gave the reason: to sit belongs to a judge, and to stand to one who is fighting, or who comes to help. Who stands with persecuted Christians? He does. It is not ye that speak.

So the question left for us is the other one. Luke ends this same discourse with it, where Mark ends with the porter. Watch ye therefore, and pray always, that ye may be accounted worthy to escape all these things that shall come to pass, and to stand before the Son of man. Watching is for standing. The question is not only whether we stand with them. It is whether, when the covering comes off, we shall be found standing where they stand.

Nobody is going to beat us in a synagogue. What is asked of us in Toronto is smaller, and for the most part we do not give it: to say what we believe when it is unwelcome, at work or at our own table; to keep Sunday; to visit the prisoner and sit with the dying; to turn the thing off and pray. A man here can be called a Christian for sixty years and never once be found one, because nothing ever came to find him. That is our danger, and our Lord named it. It is not the beasts. It is the sleep.

So look at the poster once more. I had read it as a picture of a man who needs me. He may; I have said what he is owed. But look at how he stands. He is not asking for anything. There is a crucifix on the wall behind him and a red cloth beside it, and he is in front of both, facing us, the way a witness faces a court. Pilate said, Behold the man. I think the photograph says it too, and that is my reading, and I mark it as one. I know nothing of him but what the Church that printed his picture tells me: that he is among those who have been made to stand. If so, he is nearer than I am to what Ignatius was praying to become. For a testimony against them, Mark says, and I am afraid that in Toronto them includes me.

Ad te levavi

The Mass of this Sunday has the same chants in the old books and the new, and they have been sung on this day for well over a thousand years. Read them after Mark, Ignatius and Irenaeus, and they turn out to be saying the same things, in the order a person needs them.

The introit is the first chant of the Church’s sung year. In the great medieval choir books it stood on the first page, under a painted capital A, and the first word of the year is that A: Ad te levavi animam meam, Unto thee, O LORD, do I lift up my soul. On one leaf from fourteenth-century Siena, now in London, the painter put King David inside the letter, holding up his soul to Christ; and he painted the soul as a small child. Irenaeus would have liked that. The melody does what the words say. It starts low, on Ad te, and climbs, a little at a time, until it reaches Deus meus.

The first word of the year is not a statement. It is a direction: ad, toward. That is Irenaeus’s something towards which he might advance, set to music. The year does not begin with what we know. It begins by facing someone.

The next words are the year’s first petition, and they are the prayer of someone who knows that everything is going to be uncovered. O my God, I trust in thee: let me not be ashamed. Non erubescam: let me not blush. Adam hid among the trees because he was naked. An apocalypse is an exposure; on the last day nothing is hidden. The introit does not ask to be hidden. It asks to be seen and not ashamed.

The gradual takes up the same psalm: Universi qui te exspectant non confundentur, let none that wait on thee be ashamed. Those who wait are the porter and his fellow servants. Its verse asks, Shew me thy ways, O LORD; teach me thy paths. It asks for the ways, the order of things, and not for the date.

The alleluia changes psalms and asks for one thing. Ostende nobis, Domine, misericordiam tuam: Shew us thy mercy, O LORD. Ostende is show, uncover. Of all the things the uncovering might show, this is the one the Church asks for first.

The offertory goes back to the first psalm again, Ad te, Domine, levavi animam meam, not long before the priest says Sursum corda, Lift up your hearts, and we answer that we lift them ad Dominum, to the Lord. Toward, again.

And the communion, sung while we go up to receive, answers Isaiah. He asked God to rend the heavens and come down. The communion says that the other end will open too. Dominus dabit benignitatem, et terra nostra dabit fructum suum: the LORD shall give that which is good; and our land shall yield her increase. The verse before it in the psalm says both at once: Truth shall spring out of the earth; and righteousness shall look down from heaven. We sing that the earth gives her fruit while we are given bread, which is wheat. Irenaeus saw the resurrection in it: as a cutting from the vine planted in the ground bears fruit in its season, and a grain of wheat falls into the earth and rots and rises many times over, so our bodies, fed with the Eucharist and laid in the ground, will rise in their time. Ignatius’s pure bread is in the same chant.

The new lectionary gives this Sunday a psalm the old Mass does not sing, and it is the right one. Psalm 80 calls on God as one that dwellest between the cherubims, behind the veil, and says shine forth. It asks, stir up thy strength, and come and save us; the old collect of the day, Excita, borrows its words. It prays for the vine God brought out of Egypt, and for the son of man whom thou madest strong for thyself. And its refrain is the plainest statement of what the whole season wants to see. Turn us again, O God, and cause thy face to shine; and we shall be saved. Moses was told, there shall no man see me, and live. St Paul says that God has given us the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ. What we ask to have uncovered, at the end of all of it, is a face.

Watch

So, back to the porter at the door. He is not told the hour, and he is not asked to bring the master home. He is asked to be awake. Mark has shown us, two chapters later, how badly the servants did: in every watch of that night the master was with them, and in every watch they failed him, asleep in the garden, and one of them swearing at cockcrow that he had never known him. He went on to the cross anyway. The man who denied him at cockcrow wept, and was forgiven, and kept the door of the Church after him. The sleeping of the servants did not stop the coming. It is a hard warning, and it is also a comfort.

The old Mass of this Sunday read the end of the night from St Paul. And that, knowing the time, that now it is high time to awake out of sleep: for now is our salvation nearer than when we believed. The night is far spent, the day is at hand. That is the porter’s last watch, the one called in the morning. And its Gospel, from St Luke’s telling of the same discourse, gave the posture in a sentence: look up, and lift up your heads; for your redemption draweth nigh. That is Luke’s Ad te levavi.

Advent runs from these birth pangs to a birth. Four weeks from now we will kneel at a manger, and the child in it is the anthrōpos the whole of creation is in labour with: the image, at last with a face. The Church has always said that Advent waits for two comings, the first in humility and the second in glory. It is one face both times. The child shown to the shepherds is the man Pilate showed to the crowd, and he is the one who will be seen coming in the clouds of heaven. We will know him, because we have seen him before.

And we are not yet what we shall be. It doth not yet appear. Ignatius was not yet, on the road. The clay is on the wheel. The porter is at the door in the dark, with the clocks going out around him, and nothing to do but stay awake and face the right way. St Paul’s word to the Corinthians this Sunday is for him: Who shall also confirm you unto the end, that ye may be blameless in the day of our Lord Jesus Christ. God is faithful.

He is not alone at the door. Tonight there are servants of the same house keeping the watch in places where it costs them their sleep, their land, and some of them their lives, and they are keeping it for us as well. Ask them the old question. Watchman, what of the night? And take the prophet’s answer, which is theirs: The morning cometh, and also the night. The night is not over, and the morning is not in doubt.

And what I say unto you I say unto all, Watch.
