Essays

Essay · in the register of Abraham Joshua Heschel, with St Catherine of Siena

The Spirit and the Bride Say

Thou shalt call me Ishi. A proposal to give each household one vote, cast by the husband, answered not from autonomy but from the bridal mysticism of Scripture. What Hosea’s God refuses to be called, what the English word husband means, whose consent no human power can supply, and why the Bible ends with the bride speaking and the Bridegroom saying yes.

Richard Verver · November 2026

And the Spirit and the bride say, Come. And let him that heareth say, Come.
— Revelation 22:17

One house, one vote

The proposal is small enough to fit on a ballot, and that is its danger. No one is asked to shout. A man is asked only to carry one more slip of paper on his way to the booth.

I will let its friends state it. In the summer of 2025 a pastor in Moscow, Idaho, told CNN: “In my ideal society, we would vote as households.” He added: “I would ordinarily be the one that would cast the vote, but I would cast the vote having discussed it with my household.” Another pastor of the same communion said that he would support repealing the Nineteenth Amendment. In July of this year Douglas Wilson, the best known of them, was asked on NPR why he would repeal it. “Because it’s a good idea,” he said, and laughed, and then he explained: one vote to each household, cast by its head. A woman who heads her own household, a widow or a single woman, would vote; a married woman would not, because her husband would vote for the house. A pastor in Arizona has promised that the amendment will be gone within ten years, and has written that he wants it repealed to protect the nation from women’s “suicidal empathy.”

I want to be fair to them, because a prophet who is unfair has already lost the argument he came to make. They do not say they hate women; the pastor in Arizona says he wants the amendment gone because he loves American women. Another insists that the question is not male against female but the household against the fragmentation of society. They point out that in their churches a woman who heads a household votes, as Lydia, they say, was the head of hers. Nor are they the same people as the women who bake bread on camera and sell the long dresses. That world is not of one mind: one of its first and best-known voices, an Englishwoman, writes that a traditional wife does not let herself be put where her rights are threatened, and says the movement has since been politicized and has “become its own monster”; others in it do call for repeal. When the Secretary of Defense shared the CNN segment, his own spokesman said that of course the secretary thinks women should have the right to vote. And the thing cannot easily be done: an amendment to the Constitution needs two-thirds of Congress and three-quarters of the states.

None of that makes it small. An idea does not have to win to do its work. It has only to become sayable, and then repeatable, and then the kind of thing a young man says at dinner to see who flinches. I am a canon lawyer, and a Canadian, and this is not my country’s constitution. But it is my Church’s Bible they are reading from, and my Lord’s bride they are describing, and I do not think she has been described rightly.

What they see

The prophet does not begin by denying the sickness. He begins by seeing it more clearly than the physician.

These men are right that the house is real. A marriage is not two contracts sharing a kitchen. They shall be one flesh, and from that flesh come children who did not ask to be born and must be fed every day for twenty years. The Church calls the family a domestic church, and she means it: there is a liturgy of the stove and the sickbed and the three-in-the-morning fever, and it is celebrated without vestments. I wrote of it in The Throne of His Glory as one great school of the six works, whose dignity is that nobody applauds.

They are right that an economy which counts only paid work has despised that school. It has taught a generation of women that the hours given to a child are hours lost to a career, and a generation of men that a house is a place to sleep between shifts. Some of the young women who film themselves kneading dough are protesting exactly this, and they are not wrong to protest. A world that has priced everything has made the unpriced look worthless, and the unpriced is where most love is done.

They are right that loneliness is a political fact. The man who lives alone, eats alone, votes alone and dies alone in a rented room is not the triumph of freedom. He is its casualty. The prophets never thought of a person as a sovereign atom. Israel was numbered by its families and the house of its fathers, and it stood before God as a people.

And they are right about one more thing, though they do not name it. They have seen that a soul is not made to keep itself. The self-owned individual, answerable to no one, giving nothing it has not first priced, is a small and lonely god, and our age has made millions of them. Against that god the household is a true protest: here, at least, people belong to one another.

But a true protest can be made in a false grammar. In the household they describe, one person speaks for another before the city. She may be consulted, as one of them says she would be; but the vote is his, and he casts it. They call that headship, and they mean it as service. Yet a voice that another casts for you is a voice that he holds, and the Bible has a name for the one who holds a woman and a house. Our age made the self its own owner. These men have not abolished the owner. They have moved the title from the self to the husband. And the owner’s name is the one name God has told his bride never to call him.

Ishi, not Baali

And it shall be at that day, saith the LORD, that thou shalt call me Ishi; and shalt call me no more Baali.
— Hosea 2:16

Hosea is the prophet whose wife left him, and whom God sent to love her still. Heschel read that marriage less as a sign acted out for Israel than as the education of the prophet’s own heart: in his grief Hosea came to share the grief of God. That is the root of what Heschel meant by the divine pathos. The God of Israel is not the unmoved administrator of a covenant. He is a husband who has been betrayed and will not stop loving, and the book of Hosea is the sound of it.

In the middle of that sound God says what he wants to be called.

Hebrew has two words for the man a woman marries. One is ba’al: owner, master, lord. A man is the ba’al of a field, of an ox, of a house; the ba’al ha-bayit is the master of the house. It was also the name of the storm god of Canaan, to whom Israel gave the credit for its corn and wine and oil. The other word is ish, which means simply man. It is the word the man found when he first saw the woman: she shall be called Woman, because she was taken out of Man, ishah because taken out of ish. He found a word for himself in the same breath as the word for her. One word names a possession. The other names a likeness. Ishi is my man, as ishti is my woman; the two words are mirrors.

The Bible is honest that Israel used both. Isaiah could still say thy Maker is thine husband, and his word comes from ba’al; so does Jeremiah’s. Hosea is the prophet who heard God refuse it. In that day, says the Lord, you will call me my man, and never again my owner. And then: I will betroth thee unto me in righteousness, and in judgment, and in lovingkindness, and in mercies. I will even betroth thee unto me in faithfulness: and thou shalt know the LORD. A betrothal, not a purchase; and know, the Bible’s word for the knowledge of the marriage bed, the word I followed into the wedding chamber in I Know You Not (Yet).

Genesis puts the other name in its place. He shall rule over thee is not said on the garden’s first morning. It is said after the fall, as the shape of what has gone wrong. Pope John Paul II called that rule a break and a constant threat to the unity of the two, and a threat more serious for the woman. Hosea’s Ishi is the first name, the Eden name, given back.

Now listen to our own language. The English husband does not come from ish. It comes from the Old Norse húsbóndi, the master of the house: hús, a house, and bóndi, the one who dwells on the land and works it. That is why we still say husbandry of the keeping of animals, and why the King James Bible says that Noah began to be an husbandman, and he planted a vineyard. Our word for a married man is ba’al ha-bayit in another tongue. When the King James translators came to Hosea they did not translate. They left Ishi and Baali in Hebrew, as if they knew that English had no word for the first and had already given the second to every married man. Hebrew speakers still say ba’ali, my husband, every day; some Israeli women will not, and say ishi instead, with Hosea on their side.

So the household vote is the vote of the ba’al ha-bayit. That is not an insult. It is a translation. The master of the house casts the house’s vote, as the master of a field brings in its harvest. And the God of Israel, who had every right to that name, put it away.

In the English Bible there is one Husbandman who owns the field: my Father is the husbandman. The rest of us, husbands and wives alike, are labourers together with God, and what we tend is God’s husbandry. St Matthew tells what happens when the tenants forget it. A householder plants a vineyard and lets it out to husbandmen, and when the son comes for the fruit they say, This is the heir; come, let us kill him, and let us seize on his inheritance. The sin of the wicked husbandmen is not that they worked the vineyard. It is that they began to speak of it as theirs.

Neither his nor her own

The easy answer to these men is the one I will not give. It says: her body, her vote, her life; no one owns her but herself. It is the answer of our age, and it has won most of its arguments. But it shares their grammar. It agrees that a person is the kind of thing that is owned, and quarrels only over the deed. The pastors give the title to the husband. The age gives it to the self. Neither stops to ask whether a soul is property at all.

St Paul’s answer is stranger. Ye are not your own, he tells the men of Corinth, for ye are bought with a price. It sounds like the pastors’ grammar until one asks what was paid, and why. The price was the Bridegroom’s blood, and it was paid as a ransom is paid, to set a captive free: Stand fast therefore in the liberty wherewith Christ hath made us free. The only one who ever paid for a human soul paid to free it, not to keep it. And having freed her, he asks her.

The moral law has a word for this, and Pope John Paul II used it. Obedience to God is not heteronomy, a law laid on us from outside by a stranger. It is not autonomy, a law we write for ourselves. It is participated theonomy: God’s own wisdom shared with us, so that his law becomes ours from within. That is the bride’s freedom. She is neither her husband’s nor her own. She is espoused.

No one shows it better than Catherine of Siena. She was a dyer’s daughter, and she refused both of the lives a girl of her city was allowed: the husband her family wanted and the convent veil. She stayed in her father’s house in the black and white of the Dominican penitents, and she prayed. At about twenty she was espoused to Christ. Her confessor, Raymond of Capua, tells it as she told him: the Virgin took her hand and held it out to her Son, who put a ring on it and espoused her in faith. And then he said: So now, daughter, do manfully.

The bride was told to act like a man, and she did. She left her room for the sick and the condemned. She wrote to queens and cardinals and to the captains of mercenary armies. She went to Avignon and told the pope to come home to Rome. She called him Babbo, Daddy, and wrote that she longed to see him a manly man, free from any fear, and she begged him on behalf of Christ crucified to obey. In January 1377 he was in Rome.

Under the household vote a dyer’s unmarried daughter living in her father’s house would have no voice of her own; the head of her house would carry it for her. The Church has made her a Doctor. But notice what she never claimed. She never said she belonged to herself. Her letters open in the name of Christ crucified and of sweet Mary, and she writes as servant and slave of the servants of Jesus Christ, in His precious Blood. From that place, and from no other, she told a pope what to do.

That is the voice these men cannot account for. It is not the voice of a woman who owns herself, and it is not the voice of a woman whose husband speaks for her. It is the voice of a bride who knows whose she is.

Two brides of one Bridegroom

When St Paul wrote I have espoused you to one husband, that I may present you as a chaste virgin to Christ, he was writing to the whole church at Corinth, men as much as women. St John saw a hundred and forty-four thousand men following the Lamb and called them virgins. St Bernard preached eighty-six sermons on the Song of Songs to a chapter of monks, and the bride in every one of them was the soul of each man listening. St John of the Cross wrote his Dark Night in a woman’s voice, because that is the soul’s voice before God. Before God every person is bridal. It is not a feminine trait handed to women for safekeeping. It is what a creature is when the Creator loves it.

So the husband is a bride. He stands before the Bridegroom as his wife does, with empty hands, and receives everything. And receiving is not the lower place. I said it in The Throne of His Glory and I say it again: it is the Son’s place. The Son receives everything he has from the Father and is not less. Whoever turns receiving into a rank has quarrelled with the Trinity.

Then what is a husband to his wife? St Paul tells him: love your wives, even as Christ also loved the church, and gave himself for it. He is to signify the Bridegroom’s love, which is a love that dies. He is not to take the Bridegroom’s place. A sign that takes the place of what it signifies has another name in Hosea. And Paul opens the whole teaching with a line that governs everything after it: Submitting yourselves one to another in the fear of God. John Paul II read it plainly. Between Christ and the Church the subjection runs one way; between husband and wife it is mutual.

There is a better name for a husband, and St John the Baptist gave it. He that hath the bride is the bridegroom: but the friend of the bridegroom, which standeth and heareth him, rejoiceth greatly because of the bridegroom’s voice. The greatest born of women chose the friend’s place. He stood, he listened, he rejoiced. He did not speak for the bride. He did not stand between her and the voice. He stood beside her and heard it too. That is what husband and wife are to each other: two brides of one Bridegroom, and each the Bridegroom’s friend to the other.

Dante saw where this ends. In the white rose of Paradise every soul looks on God, and near and far, he says, there neither add nor take away, because where God governs with no one in between, the laws of nature have no say. No soul stands in front of another. Christ said that in the resurrection they neither marry, nor are given in marriage, not because love is undone but because nothing stands between any soul and its God any more, and so every soul is free to be given to every other. Marriage is the school of that, here and now. It is not the place where one soul shades another from the light.

The household vote asks a husband to stand where the friend of the bridegroom never stands: in front of the bride, speaking.

A yes no one else can say

The angel Gabriel was sent from God unto a city of Galilee, named Nazareth, to a virgin espoused to a man whose name was Joseph. She was betrothed. By every custom of her people Joseph had a claim on her, and her father’s house had a say. The angel went to neither. He came in unto her. And the Word through whom all things were made waited on the answer of a girl in Nazareth until she gave it: Behold the handmaid of the Lord; be it unto me according to thy word. St Bernard pictured the whole world at that moment on its knees before her, Adam and all his children with it, waiting for her to speak. The God who could have taken asked. Then she sang about it: He hath put down the mighty from their seats, and exalted them of low degree.

The Church’s law has kept that moment better than her preachers sometimes have. It says that marriage is made by the consent of the parties, and that this consent cannot be supplied by any human power: not the father’s, not the bishop’s, not the pope’s. A marriage entered through force or grave fear from outside is no marriage at all. The Church’s tribunals spend most of their working lives asking one question about a wedding. Did she say yes, herself? And did he?

I owe the pastors their strongest text, and it is in the Law. In Numbers a woman’s vow, made in her father’s house in her youth, could be disallowed by her father on the day he heard it, and a wife’s by her husband; but every vow of a widow, and of her that is divorced … shall stand against her. That is the household vote almost exactly, long before anyone thought of a ballot. St Thomas read the chapter and held that a wife’s vow, in what lay in her husband’s power, stood only if he did not gainsay it. The Church does not legislate so now. Her present law says that of its nature a vow binds only the one who makes it. A husband whose rights a vow touches may suspend its obligation for as long as keeping it harms him, and no longer; a wife may do the same with his. He cannot unmake his wife’s promise to God.

And in the same book, three chapters earlier, the Law bent the other way, and God bent it. Five sisters, Mahlah, Noah, and Hoglah, and Milcah, and Tirzah, whose father had died with no son, stood before Moses, and before Eleazar the priest, and before the princes and all the congregation, by the door of the tabernacle, and asked for their father’s inheritance. Theirs was a house with no man at its head, the very case the household vote is built for. They did not find a man to speak for them. They spoke. Moses did not know the answer, and brought their cause before the LORD. And the Lord said: The daughters of Zelophehad speak right. The law of Israel was amended on the petition of five women, and God himself gave the judgment. I am a canonist, and I know few finer pages in the history of law.

Abigail was married to a man whose name means fool. When he insulted David, and David came for his house with four hundred men, she told not her husband Nabal. She rode out with bread and wine and figs and met David on the road and turned him back from murder, and David said: blessed be thy advice, and blessed be thou, which hast kept me this day from coming to shed blood. Had her house spoken through its head, the men of her house would have been dead by morning. Deborah was the wife of Lapidoth, and she judged Israel at that time. When the book of the law was found in the temple, the king’s priest went to Huldah, the wife of Shallum, to learn what it meant.

The woman these men hold up as the pattern of a wife, the woman of Proverbs, considereth a field, and buyeth it. Her husband is known in the gates, when he sitteth among the elders of the land: the gates were where the elders met and judged, the city’s council. And the poem ends: let her own works praise her in the gates. He sits there; she is praised there, in her own name, for her own works. At Pentecost Peter said the last days had come, because God was pouring out his Spirit upon all flesh: and your sons and your daughters shall prophesy, and on my servants and on my handmaidens. Not on the heads of households. On all flesh.

The Church has said it in her own voice. In 1945, with Europe in ruins, Pius XII told the women of Italy that their day had come, that public life needed them, and that to vote was a strict obligation of conscience. Twenty years later the Council called every discrimination based on sex contrary to God’s intent, and named among its victims the woman who is not free to choose her own husband. Her law opens its account of the baptized with a true equality among them in dignity and action, and it gives every lay person the freedom that belongs to every citizen in the affairs of the earthly city.

The canon on consent is about marriage, not elections, and I will not pretend it settles a question of American law. But it shows what the Church thinks of one person’s yes. If no human power can supply a woman’s consent to one man, I do not see how one man can supply her judgment of the common good.

Before him shall be gathered all nations

In The Throne of His Glory I followed St Matthew to the last judgment. Before him shall be gathered all nations: and he shall separate them one from another, as a shepherd divideth his sheep from the goats. One from another: not house from house. The King does not ask who was the head of the household. He asks each one what she did for the hungry and the stranger and the man in prison. Ezekiel had said it centuries before, to a people who thought they could carry their fathers’ guilt and their fathers’ credit: The son shall not bear the iniquity of the father, neither shall the father bear the iniquity of the son. And St Paul: every one of us shall give account of himself to God.

Heschel said that what the prophets teach first about any people can be put in seven words: “Few are guilty, but all are responsible.” A vote is one of the small, real ways a citizen bears that responsibility. It is her hand, however small, on whether the city feeds the hungry or sends them away, whether it takes the stranger in, whether the grandmother at the federal building is given water or a plate of ice. To take a woman’s vote does not take her responsibility. Nothing can. It leaves her responsible and silent, answerable for a city she was not allowed to speak to.

The household vote promises that the head of the house will answer for the house. No one will. At the throne of glory there are no proxies. The husband will not be able to say, I voted for her, and the wife will not be asked what her husband did. She will be asked what she did. So the question before the city is not whether a woman should be given a voice. God has already given her one, and he will ask her what she did with it. The question is only whether the city will let her say aloud what the King will ask her anyway.

One of the pastors fears what he calls women’s suicidal empathy. Prudence will have its say in the city, and it should; the city is where prudence lives. But I notice what the King asks about from his throne. He does not ask who was prudent. He asks who fed him, who gave him drink, who took him in when he was a stranger, who clothed him and came to him sick and in prison. Every question he puts is a question of mercy, and he names himself in the one who needed it. Whatever else may be said for the household vote, it was not proposed in order to give him more of that.

The Spirit and the bride say, Come

The Bible ends in conversation. And the Spirit and the bride say, Come. And let him that heareth say, Come. And let him that is athirst come. Three verses later the Bridegroom answers: Surely I come quickly. And the Church answers him: Amen. Even so, come, Lord Jesus.

Notice who speaks. The bride has no head of household but God, and she does not wait for anyone to speak for her. She speaks, and the Spirit speaks with her in the same breath: the Spirit who was poured out on the handmaidens at Pentecost is in her mouth at the end of the world. Her last word in Scripture is not a petition passed up through an intermediary. It is an imperative, spoken to God himself: Come. He does not rebuke her for it. He says yes.

The Song of Songs ends the same way. Thou that dwellest in the gardens, the companions hearken to thy voice: cause me to hear it. The Beloved asks to hear her voice, and she answers him: Make haste, my beloved. Earlier he had said it plainly: let me hear thy voice; for sweet is thy voice. The Bridegroom of every soul wants nothing so much as to hear her speak.

That is the bridal mysticism these men have half seen. They are right that a soul is made to be given and not kept. They are wrong about to whom she is given, and how. She is given to God, and God does not take her. He asks, and waits, and answers. A husband who loves his wife as Christ loves the Church will want what Christ wants. He will want to hear her voice: in the house, in the gates, and in the city.

So I say to the pastors, with something of Catherine’s nerve and none of her sanctity: be men. Be ish, not ba’al. Stand beside your wives as the friend of the Bridegroom stands, and listen for the voice that is sweet to him. And to the women of their churches, and of mine, I say what Peter said at Pentecost: your daughters shall prophesy. Speak.

The Spirit and the bride say, Come. Let no one say it for her.
