The Benedict Year

Homeschool curriculum

For parents

Read every essay yourself before your child does. They were written for adults, and several of them need a decision from you before they are read aloud.

Books and materials

  • Trenton Lee Stewart’s novels: The Mysterious Benedict Society (Term 1), The Extraordinary Education of Nicholas Benedict (Term 2) and The Mysterious Benedict Society and the Prisoner’s Dilemma (Term 3). The Perilous Journey and The Riddle of Ages are for the summer.
  • Mr. Benedict’s Book of Perplexing Puzzles, Elusive Enigmas, and Curious Conundrums, for Monday puzzles beyond the ones each unit suggests.
  • The essays, printed; each has a PDF on its page.
  • A Bible and the Compendium of the Catechism of the Catholic Church.
  • A commonplace book for each child: every Friday’s answers and every unit’s essay, the year in one notebook.
  • For the weeks with things to make: two flashlights, a pendulum (string, a heavy nut and a lazy Susan), a shoebox for a camera obscura, modelling clay, graph paper and painter’s tape.

Mr. Benedict’s tests

The year is assessed the way Mr. Benedict chose his children: by how they think, whether they tell the truth, and how they work together, never by how much they can recite. He held that what let them resist the hidden messages was an unusual love of truth, so that is what every test looks for.

Every Friday

  1. A question hidden in a question. One item cannot be answered without noticing something in another, as on his second test.
  2. An honesty question. One question where the honest answer costs something, as “Are you brave?” did. Full marks for the true answer, whatever it is.
  3. A team problem. Solved together, with each child’s gift needed. There is always a way that uses everyone.
  4. Spot the Whisperer. An advertisement, headline or slogan: what does it want you to feel, what does it want you to do, and what message sits under the one it says?

Answers go in the commonplace book. The reasons are marked, not the recitation.

The end-of-term trials, in weeks 12, 24 and 36

  • The written test. Ten questions: three with a trick in the wording, one whose answer appears in another question, and one honesty question.
  • The tile room. A problem that gives way to looking rather than computing, like the floor of rectangles.
  • The maze. A route learned with the eyes open and walked again with them shut: memory and order.
  • The offered answers. Partway through, someone offers the answers. The test is passed by refusing them.
  • The essay. One page in a borrowed voice on the term’s best question. In Term 3 it is the essay the child would add to the map.

The twelve cases

Each unit’s third week works one case. They were worked through a claim checker, which does not say whether a claim is true; it shows when a claim is badly formed: a fact treated as a doctrine, a law treated as a teaching, a verdict said of a person instead of an act. One of its rules is a good one to say aloud: never compute anyone’s interior.
