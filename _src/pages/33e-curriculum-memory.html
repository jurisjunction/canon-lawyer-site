Week 1 · the kind of answer

One timeline for Mr. Benedict’s whole life, from the prequel through the first book: put the events in order first, then lay on the dates you can find. Monday puzzle: anagrams, the kind Nicholas uses.

Week 2 · the essay

Half of Nothing, in the register of St Jerome. Retell the banquet, and the paragraph after it, in your own words, and read the rest aloud with a map. Then put its events in order, from Herod the Great to Rome’s fall to Alaric, before dating any of them. Ask: why is it so hard to take something back in front of other people?

Week 3 · the case

A forged letter. A letter in an old collection is said to be by a pope who died about the year 160, yet it quotes a council held in 325. The attribution fails on the dates alone: a dead man does not quote a later council. (The case is modelled on the False Decretals, which were exposed in 1628.) Keep two questions apart: who wrote a text, and whether it binds. A text in a collection the Church promulgated binds from that promulgation, whoever wrote it; a private collection gives it no force of its own. Then the game: the parent writes three “old letters”, one with something in it that could not have been known yet, and the child finds it.

The end first

Ideas have families too. Draw a family of thinkers from Thomas to Kant, with an arrow from each to those who learned from him, and mark which kinds of “for” each one still keeps. It is not a tree: Locke has four parents. On one reading of each, Bacon dropped ends in nature, Hobbes kept ends only as desire, and Leibniz took ends in nature back.
